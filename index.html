Headquarters and foreign subsidiaries: what employees of the same company, in the same job, say about their work

Glassdoor reviews of S&P 500 multinationals, 2008–2025. Each pros and cons text was coded into 126 themes. Employees at headquarters (HQ) are compared with employees of the same company holding the same job title abroad. We do not yet know where the theoretically important differences are, so every available dimension is shown.

Data and method

Texts. Reviews with an identified workplace country (1.83M) of S&P 500 firms. The coded sample holds 579,240 pros/cons texts from 527,469 reviews: a random 10.8% of all distinct texts plus a stratified sample, combined with design weights (Horvitz–Thompson). Coding by an LLM (GPT‑6 Luna) against a 126‑theme codebook built by computational grounded theory (BERTopic topics → codebook → saturation runs; 95.5% coverage). Agreement with two stronger models on 970 held-out reviews: F1 ≈ 0.80, equal for HQ and subsidiary texts.

Matching. A cell is one company × normalised job title. It enters when it has at least 5 coded pros and 5 coded cons texts at HQ and abroad. Cells count equally. HQ = the country of the company's headquarters; subsidiary = any other country.

Measures (all in percentage points, subsidiary minus HQ):

  • Praise rate: share of pros texts that mention the theme.
  • Complaint rate: share of cons texts that mention it.
  • Net difference = (praise − complaint abroad) − (praise − complaint at HQ). Positive = better abroad, negative = better at HQ.

95% intervals come from a company-cluster bootstrap (500 draws). A domain score counts a text when it mentions any theme of that domain. Star ratings (overall and six sub-ratings, recommend, CEO approval, outlook) use all reviews with an identified country, matched the same way with at least 5 reviews per side.

Dimensions. Splitting the subsidiary side only: region, country, the border test. Splitting both sides: HQ origin, sector, job level, job function (keywords in the title), period, employment status, tenure, and current vs former employee. Groups with fewer than 30 matched cells are faded.

Possible theoretical readings

Offered to the experts as starting points, not conclusions. Reading A was proposed by a GPT‑6 co‑reviewer from the overall tables; Reading B is ours, prompted by the dimension patterns above. The references are classic works that each reading would build on.

Reading A

Selective geographic differentiation inside the multinational

Integration does not produce one employee experience. Domains tied to national institutions and to corporate resource allocation (benefits) favour HQ. Domains shaped by local interaction and by distance from corporate processes (culture, management, change) favour subsidiaries. The split appears at the national border, not with distance from HQ. The contribution moves the transfer-of-practices literature from formal HR practices to employees' experiences.

  • Institutional duality and local responsiveness
  • Internal labour markets segmented by location
  • HQ–subsidiary power and the politics of practice transfer

Rosenzweig & Nohria 1994, JIBS · Kostova 1999, AMR · Bartlett & Ghoshal 1989, Managing Across Borders · Doeringer & Piore 1971, Internal Labor Markets · Edwards, Colling & Ferner 2007, HRMJ · Ferner 1997, HRMJ

Reading B

Two reference points: the local labour market abroad and the corporate centre at home

1. Local comparison abroad. Subsidiary employees judge the multinational against local employers, so its relative standing depends on the host labour market. The star-rating advantage abroad is near zero in Australia, Ireland, the UK, the Netherlands and Hong Kong. It is large in Mexico, the Philippines, Brazil, China, India, Poland and Malaysia, and the same countries carry most of the culture gap. This is consistent with a foreign-employer premium, and with social comparison against local alternatives.

2. Costs of centrality at home. HQ employees complain more about strategy, restructuring, layoffs, scale, politics and leadership. Praise for these themes barely differs. HQ staff seem exposed to corporate decision-making that subsidiaries are buffered from. In return they praise colleagues' expertise, internal mobility, the value of the employer on a CV and interesting work more often: HQ as the talent and career hub.

3. Benefits as institutional salience. The benefits gap is a gap in praise, while the compensation-and-benefits star rating is not lower abroad. Employer benefits matter more to employees in the US, a liberal market economy, than where the state provides them. The gap disappears for other US states and for part-time staff, and grows with tenure.

Festinger 1954, Human Relations · Adams 1965, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology · Kahneman & Tversky 1979, Econometrica · Aitken, Harrison & Lipsey 1996, J. of International Economics · Thompson 1967, Organizations in Action · Birkinshaw & Hood 1998, AMR · Andersson, Forsgren & Holm 2002, SMJ · Hall & Soskice 2001, Varieties of Capitalism

Questions for the experts

  • Which pattern is the finding: benefits vs culture, the complaint-driven HQ disadvantage in organisational themes, or the host-country gradient?
  • Is the border-versus-distance contrast a sufficient identification device, or does it need same-cell designs (company × job × year)?
  • Should the outcome be framed as experienced working conditions, or as evaluative salience (what employees choose to mention)?

Caveats

  • LLM coding error is not yet corrected. The design-based correction (Egami et al. 2023) with the double-coded sample is planned. Agreement does not differ between HQ and subsidiary texts.
  • All coded texts are in English. Non-English reviews are few (about 50 rating cells) and are not in the coded sample.
  • Job-function groups come from keywords in the title and are approximate. Europe-headquartered firms have only 17 text cells.
  • "No cons" is written twice as often abroad (5.9% vs 3.2% of cons texts). It lowers the net score mechanically and reflects reporting style; read it separately.
  • Glassdoor reviewers self-select. Matching on company and job title removes firm and occupation composition, but not who chooses to write.
  • The 126 tests are not adjusted for multiplicity. With about 52 of 141 intervals excluding zero, far more than chance, the pattern is real overall. Single small themes still need care.
