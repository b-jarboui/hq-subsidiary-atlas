WORKING CONDITIONS AT MULTINATIONAL HEADQUARTERS AND FOREIGN SUBSIDIARIES

A multidimensional, matched-company and matched-job investigation

Research report for discussion with international business, international HRM, and management scholars

Data source: atlas_results.xlsx
Observation period: 2008–2025
Research question: Do employees experience different working conditions at a multinational enterprise's headquarters and its foreign subsidiaries, when comparing the same company and the same job?

PART I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY, RESEARCH DESIGN, AND THE CORE EMPIRICAL FINDINGS

1. Executive summary

1.1. The central discovery: foreign subsidiaries appear to offer a different bundle of employment advantages

The results reveal a potentially important and theoretically interesting phenomenon.

Employees in foreign subsidiaries report more favorable organizational and relational experiences than comparable employees in headquarters locations, but they report less favorable benefits.

This is not simply a general difference in employee satisfaction. The advantages and disadvantages concern different components of the employment relationship.

Three results are particularly striking.

First, foreign subsidiaries have substantially fewer complaints about management, organizational functioning, and workplace relationships.

Across 1,011 matched company-job cells representing 156 companies:

  • Management and employee relations: +6.75 percentage points in the net measure favoring subsidiaries, with a 95% confidence interval of [4.92, 8.78].
  • Organization and business: +6.98 pp, 95% CI [5.19, 9.18].
  • Culture and relationships: +5.43 pp, 95% CI [3.70, 7.20].

These differences are economically meaningful within the text-based measures and statistically distinguishable from zero.

Importantly, the findings are not driven exclusively by employees praising subsidiaries more frequently.

For management and employee relations, praise is essentially identical between headquarters and subsidiaries:

  • HQ praise: 15.12%.
  • Subsidiary praise: 15.14%.
  • Difference: +0.02 pp.

However, complaints are substantially less frequent:

  • HQ complaints: 28.89%.
  • Subsidiary complaints: 22.16%.
  • Difference: −6.73 pp.

This suggests that the relative advantage is primarily associated with the lower reporting of negative management experiences, rather than an across-the-board increase in positive descriptions.

A similar mechanism appears in organizational functioning.

Second, subsidiaries have a pronounced disadvantage in employee benefits.

The overall benefits domain exhibits:

  • HQ praise: 32.13%.
  • Subsidiary praise: 23.69%.
  • Difference: −8.44 pp.
  • HQ complaints: 6.22%.
  • Subsidiary complaints: 4.40%.
  • Difference: −1.82 pp.
  • Net difference: −6.62 pp, 95% CI [−8.76, −4.24].

The benefits result is unusual because subsidiaries report fewer benefits complaints, even while reporting substantially less benefits praise.

Therefore, it would be misleading to interpret the result as straightforward evidence that subsidiary employees are more dissatisfied with their benefits.

Rather, benefits are less frequently described positively in subsidiaries.

This may reflect actual differences in employment packages, national institutional arrangements, employee expectations, or differences in the propensity to mention benefits.

Distinguishing these explanations is essential.

Third, employees in subsidiaries give higher overall Glassdoor ratings.

Using the separate, substantially larger matched rating sample:

  • HQ overall rating: 3.679/5.
  • Subsidiary overall rating: 3.878/5.
  • Difference: +0.199 rating points, 95% CI [0.168, 0.225].

This discrepancy deserves particular attention.

It suggests that the frequency with which employees mention an employment characteristic is not equivalent to their evaluation of that characteristic.

The combination of narrative and rating evidence is considerably more informative than either source alone.

Fourth, the confidence intervals represent within-group uncertainty. They do not themselves establish that two regional coefficients differ significantly from each other.

Nevertheless, the contrasts are sufficiently pronounced to motivate formal interaction tests.

1.3. The geographic results point toward a potentially stronger theoretical contribution

The particularly interesting result is not merely that subsidiaries have better reported management experiences.

It is that the same multinational may be experienced differently depending on where its organizational practices are implemented.

This raises an important theoretical possibility.

A multinational may maintain a recognizable corporate identity while generating systematically different employee experiences across its organizational locations.

Such differences could arise through several mechanisms:

  1. Organizational centrality: Headquarters employees may be more directly exposed to corporate restructuring, strategic uncertainty, political competition, and complex decision processes.

  2. Local adaptation: Foreign subsidiaries may modify employment practices, managerial relationships, and workplace arrangements to accommodate local institutions and expectations.

  3. Selection and employer prestige: Employment in a multinational subsidiary may provide different status, opportunities, and expectations relative to the domestic employment market.

  4. Institutional differences: Employees operating under different labor-market regulations and social-protection systems may report different employment advantages and disadvantages.

  5. Reference-group effects: Employees may evaluate the same multinational relative to their respective local alternatives.

  6. Organizational buffering: Subsidiary employees may be less directly exposed to some headquarters-level strategic tensions, although they may simultaneously confront disadvantages associated with geographic distance and peripheral status.

These interpretations are theoretically plausible but should not yet be treated as demonstrated mechanisms.

The existing data identify differences in reported experiences, not the causal processes generating those differences.

1.5. The evidence does not support a simple headquarters-versus-subsidiary hierarchy

A conventional interpretation might expect headquarters jobs to be superior because headquarters employees are closer to corporate decision-making, senior executives, internal opportunities, and organizational resources.

An alternative view might expect foreign subsidiaries to offer more attractive local employment packages because multinationals can operate as high-status employers in host labor markets.

The results do not consistently support either simple prediction.

Instead, they indicate a differentiated employment experience:

Headquarters advantages

  • More positive references to benefits.
  • More frequent praise of colleague expertise.
  • Greater positive references to certain internal career opportunities.
  • More positive references to aspects of work content.

Subsidiary advantages

  • More positive evaluations of workplace culture.
  • Fewer complaints about management.
  • Fewer complaints about organizational change and instability.
  • Fewer complaints about organizational complexity and coordination.
  • More positive references to learning through work.
  • Higher overall ratings, especially culture and senior management.

Mixed or conditional differences

  • Compensation.
  • Working time.
  • Workload.
  • Career opportunities.
  • Work arrangements.
  • Technology.
  • Physical working conditions.

This differentiated pattern is more interesting theoretically than a simple ranking.

It suggests that multinational employment quality may consist of partially competing dimensions.

1.6. The strongest candidate finding

At this stage, I would provisionally formulate the leading finding as follows:

The multinational employment paradox: Employees in foreign subsidiaries report more favorable managerial and relational experiences, despite headquarters employees reporting stronger benefits advantages. These differences occur within matched company-job comparisons and vary substantially across geographic and occupational contexts.

The word paradox is provisional.

There may be no actual contradiction if monetary and nonmonetary employment conditions are determined by different institutional and organizational processes.

Indeed, that separation may ultimately become the theoretical contribution.

A potentially stronger interpretation is a multidimensional headquarters-subsidiary employment asymmetry.

Under this interpretation, the location of an employee within a multinational is associated with different configurations of:

  • Material employment rewards.
  • Relational and managerial experiences.
  • Organizational exposure.
  • Career development.
  • Work demands.

The purpose of the remaining analysis is to establish which dimensions are robust, which depend on geographic or occupational context, and which may be measurement artifacts.

2. Data and method

2.1. Data source

The study draws on Glassdoor employee reviews associated with S&P 500 firms during 2008–2025.

The underlying dataset contains approximately 1.83 million reviews with identified workplace countries.

The coded analytical sample comprises:

  • 579,240 pros/cons texts, originating from
  • 527,469 employee reviews.

The sample combines a random sample of 10.8% of distinct texts with an additional stratified sample.

Horvitz-Thompson design weights are used to account for the sampling design.

The observations analyzed in the text-based results are English-language texts.

Consequently, the analysis should not be interpreted as representing employees who write reviews in all languages.

2.2. Theme identification and classification

The classification system contains:

  • 126 individual themes.
  • 15 broader domains.

The thematic framework was developed using a computational grounded-theory approach informed by BERTopic topic discovery.

The README reports that thematic saturation was reached, with approximately 95.5% coverage.

An LLM, identified in the documentation as GPT-6 Luna, then classified the review texts.

Coding agreement was evaluated against classifications produced by Claude Opus and GPT-6.1 Sol.

On 970 held-out reviews, the reported F1 score was approximately 0.80, with similar agreement for headquarters and subsidiary texts.

This is encouraging evidence regarding differential measurement quality.

However, similar coding agreement across headquarters and subsidiaries does not establish the absence of systematic classification bias.

For instance, a classifier might treat culturally different descriptions of management, working conditions, or employee benefits differently while maintaining similar aggregate F1 scores.

The README explicitly notes that a subsequent correction for LLM measurement error is planned using the design-based supervised-learning approach associated with Egami and colleagues (2023).

Until that correction is implemented, the reported intervals do not incorporate all relevant classification uncertainty.

2.3. Matching design

The principal comparison is conducted at the level of:

Company × normalized job title.

A matched cell enters the coded-text analysis when it contains at least:

  • Five coded pros texts at headquarters.
  • Five coded cons texts at headquarters.
  • Five coded pros texts at the subsidiary location.
  • Five coded cons texts at the subsidiary location.

Matched cells receive equal weight.

The overall coded comparison contains:

  • 1,011 matched cells.
  • 156 companies.

Matching employees by company and normalized job title offers an important advantage over conventional cross-country comparisons.

It holds constant important elements of organizational identity and nominal occupation.

However, job-title matching does not guarantee equivalence in:

  • Actual responsibilities.
  • Seniority.
  • Decision authority.
  • Work intensity.
  • Organizational reporting relationships.
  • Project complexity.
  • Employment contract.
  • Compensation grade.

The comparisons are therefore more appropriately described as within-company, matched-title differences rather than definitively identical jobs.

2.4. Outcome construction

Each review contains positive and negative text fields.

The classification system identifies whether a particular theme is mentioned within each field.

For theme kk, let:

PHQ,kP_{HQ,k}

represent the percentage of headquarters pros texts mentioning that theme.

Similarly:

PSUB,kP_{SUB,k}

represents the corresponding subsidiary percentage.

The praise difference is:

ΔPk=PSUB,k−PHQ,k\Delta P_k=P_{SUB,k}-P_{HQ,k}

The complaint difference is:

ΔCk=CSUB,k−CHQ,k\Delta C_k=C_{SUB,k}-C_{HQ,k}

The principal composite measure is:

ΔNETk=ΔPk−ΔCk\Delta NET_k=\Delta P_k-\Delta C_k

All text differences are reported in percentage points.

A positive net difference indicates that the subsidiary has a more favorable balance of praise and complaint mentions.

A negative difference indicates the reverse.

Important interpretation

The net measure is not a direct estimate of the difference in the probability that an employee is satisfied.

The praise and complaint rates have separate denominators.

Consequently, subtraction produces a useful descriptive contrast in relative thematic emphasis, but it does not create an absolute satisfaction scale.

For this reason, praise and complaint results should always be reported separately.

This distinction is particularly important because some of the strongest results arise from fewer complaints rather than greater praise.

2.5. Domain construction

A domain outcome is positive when a text mentions at least one constituent theme.

For example, the management and employee relations domain includes themes relating to management quality, leadership, recognition, employee treatment, and other managerial processes.

Domain frequencies are therefore not the arithmetic sums of individual theme frequencies.

A text mentioning multiple themes in the same domain is counted once for that domain.

2.6. Statistical uncertainty

The reported confidence intervals are based on:

  • Company-cluster bootstrap estimation.
  • 500 bootstrap replications.
  • Nominal 95% confidence intervals.

Company clustering is important because matched cells within the same multinational are unlikely to be statistically independent.

The workbook also reports the percentage of matched cells in which the subsidiary's net measure exceeds the headquarters measure.

That statistic provides useful information about the distribution of differences across matched cells.

However, a positive average should not automatically be interpreted as a majority advantage.

Similarly, the intervals do not account for the full set of potential uncertainties relating to theme discovery, classification errors, and multiple testing.

2.7. Rating analysis

A separate analysis uses star ratings from all qualifying Glassdoor reviews with identified workplace countries.

The matching criterion requires at least five reviews on each side of the comparison.

The overall rating analysis contains:

  • 6,761 matched cells.
  • 286 companies.

This sample is substantially larger than the coded-text sample.

The rating results are therefore valuable for external consistency within the study, but they are not strictly parallel estimates from identical matched cells.

A difference between text and rating results could reflect either:

  1. Genuine differences between thematic narratives and overall evaluations; or
  2. Differences in sample composition.

An eventual robustness analysis should restrict both analyses to their common matched cells.

2.8. Analytical dimensions

The workbook supports comparisons across:

  1. Subsidiary region.
  2. Subsidiary country.
  3. Domestic-versus-international border crossing.
  4. Headquarters region of origin.
  5. Industry sector.
  6. Job level.
  7. Job function.
  8. Historical period.
  9. Employment status.
  10. Employee tenure.
  11. Current versus former employment status.

In addition, the company-level sheet permits examination of heterogeneity across individual multinationals.

The README advises that groups containing fewer than 30 matched cells should be considered unreliable.

This threshold is adopted as a minimum screening criterion, not as proof that all larger groups produce stable estimates.

2.9. Important limitations

Four limitations deserve emphasis before discussing the results.

First, the study is observational. It can identify systematic associations but cannot yet establish that working at headquarters or abroad causes specific employment outcomes.

Second, Glassdoor contributors are self-selected. People who write reviews may differ systematically across countries, occupations, and employment statuses.

Third, theme mentions are not equivalent to objective working conditions. Differences may reflect actual employment practices, perceived importance, local norms, or reporting behavior.

Fourth, multiple testing has not been adjusted. The study examines a large number of themes and subgroups, making some apparently significant results likely by chance.

In evaluating the findings, I therefore prioritize:

  • Large effect sizes.
  • Confidence intervals excluding zero.
  • Substantive coherence across related themes.
  • Consistency across independent subgroups.
  • Agreement with star ratings.
  • Stability in samples containing many companies and matched cells.

Conversely, isolated findings in small subgroups receive limited interpretive weight.

Observation 2: Subsidiary advantages are frequently complaint-driven

  • Net difference: +6.98 pp.

Thus, subsidiaries actually receive less positive organizational commentary.

This is essential.

  • Praise difference: +1.91 pp.

Observation 3: Benefits display the opposite asymmetry

Benefits are less frequently praised in subsidiaries, but also less frequently criticized.

The negative net difference therefore arises from the imbalance between reductions in praise and reductions in complaints.

This implies that the overall pattern cannot be interpreted through a single general mechanism of employee positivity.

Observation 4: Compensation is not uniformly worse in subsidiaries

Although the benefits domain shows a substantial headquarters advantage, compensation does not.

Its net difference is −1.32 pp, with a confidence interval covering zero.

This distinction is theoretically consequential.

It suggests separating:

  • Direct monetary compensation.
  • Nonwage benefits.
  • Relational employment quality.
  • Career development.
  • Work conditions.

Combining these into a single employment-quality index could conceal the central contribution.

The results are consistent with the possibility that a subsidiary workplace can offer a favorable organizational atmosphere despite possessing a different configuration of professional expertise and employee networks.

The findings also indicate that organizational culture should not be treated as a uniform firm-wide attribute.

A shared corporate identity may coexist with locally distinctive workplace climates.

This is potentially important for international business theory, particularly research on the relationship between multinational integration and local adaptation.

That distinction should become a central topic in the theory and robustness sections.

However, the observed difference could reflect local labor-market alternatives, selection into reviewing, or unequal exposure to corporate restructuring.

Headquarters roles may require employees to navigate a larger number of organizational interfaces, reporting relationships, and strategic decisions.

The direction of the evidence is therefore theoretically coherent, although not conclusive.

3.4.4. A potentially important distinction: organizational knowledge versus organizational experience

An alternative explanation is that headquarters employees are simply more aware of organizational problems.

For example, an employee working close to corporate decision-making may mention restructuring, strategy, and financial instability more often even if their own immediate working conditions are not objectively worse.

Consequently, the organization domain may partly measure proximity to corporate information and decision-making.

This possibility is especially important because headquarters employees also report more praise for certain aspects of colleague expertise and organizational resources.

The combination may reflect greater corporate exposure, with both its benefits and costs.

Importantly, complaints also increase.

Internal career mobility shows:

  • Praise difference: −1.65 pp.
  • Complaint difference: −0.63 pp.
  • Net difference: −1.02 pp, 95% CI [−1.72, −0.37].

This is consistent with the possibility that headquarters provide greater access to internal career networks and opportunities.

However, actual promotion rates or mobility outcomes are not measured.

3.6.3. A useful theoretical distinction

The results suggest differentiating:

  1. Learning opportunities within the current job.
  2. Mobility opportunities within the multinational.

These are not equivalent.

Subsidiary employees may gain valuable learning experiences while headquarters employees enjoy greater access to internal mobility networks.

If confirmed, this would provide a more nuanced interpretation of the relationship between organizational centrality and employee development.

3.6.4. Comparison with ratings

The Career Opportunities rating favors subsidiaries by +0.135 points.

This appears inconsistent with the headquarters advantage in internal mobility.

However, it is compatible with the subsidiary advantage in learning through work.

The broader rating may capture multiple career-related experiences.

The important conclusion is therefore that career development is multidimensional and should not be represented by a single measure.

3.9.6. General responses and the NO_CONS artifact

This domain requires particular caution.

The theme NO_CONS, indicating that a reviewer wrote something equivalent to no cons, is more frequent in subsidiary complaint texts:

  • HQ: 3.24%.
  • Subsidiaries: 5.90%.
  • Difference: +2.66 pp.
  • 95% CI [2.03, 3.31].

The resulting net difference is −2.74 pp.

However, this is not evidence that subsidiaries have worse working conditions.

Indeed, writing no cons is usually an indication that the reviewer did not identify a negative aspect to report.

Treating it as an ordinary negative complaint theme would create an interpretation problem.

NO_CONS should be treated as a reporting-style indicator, not a substantive working-condition complaint.

Its higher incidence abroad may indicate differences in review-writing conventions, satisfaction, or both.

A particularly important additional test is whether the substantive domain differences remain stable after accounting for the unequal frequency of no-cons statements.

This is one of the highest-priority measurement robustness exercises.

  • Culture & Values ratings.
  • Senior Management ratings.

Finding B: A headquarters advantage in benefits praise

This is supported by:

  • Overall benefits.
  • Leave entitlements.
  • Retirement benefits.
  • Education assistance.

However, the simultaneous reduction in complaints abroad complicates a simple welfare interpretation.

Finding C: Greater headquarters exposure to organizational problems

This is supported by:

  • Job-security concerns.
  • Organizational structure.
  • Corporate strategy.
  • Bureaucracy and coordination.

The next analytical task is to distinguish actual exposure to organizational disadvantages from differences in information and reporting.

Finding D: Substantial heterogeneity beneath the overall averages

Compensation, working time, workload, learning, and work content exhibit relatively weak or inconclusive aggregate differences.

However, the subgroup results already indicate substantial geographic and occupational reversals.

These interactions may ultimately prove more theoretically informative than the aggregate differences themselves.

PART II. GEOGRAPHIC HETEROGENEITY AND THE HEADQUARTERS BORDER TEST

5. The geographic dimension: where does the subsidiary advantage originate?

The overall results establish that foreign subsidiaries report more favorable managerial and organizational experiences, while headquarters employees more frequently praise their benefits.

However, these aggregate relationships conceal substantial geographical heterogeneity.

This heterogeneity is potentially more important theoretically than the average headquarters-subsidiary difference.

If foreign subsidiaries consistently differed from headquarters in the same way across countries, a general explanation based on organizational location might be sufficient.

Instead, the evidence indicates that the direction, magnitude, and composition of the headquarters-subsidiary gap depend substantially on the subsidiary's geographic location.

Three patterns deserve particular attention:

  1. Asia exhibits a pronounced managerial and organizational advantage, alongside a substantial benefits disadvantage.
  2. Europe exhibits a particularly strong cultural advantage, but a much weaker managerial advantage and less favorable working-time and coordination outcomes.
  3. North America exhibits relatively limited organizational differences, suggesting that geographic and institutional context may matter more than subsidiary status alone.

The differences are not merely differences in the magnitude of a common subsidiary advantage. Some outcomes actually reverse direction.

This section examines all 15 domains across geographic regions and countries, retaining the distinction between positive and negative review narratives.

Interpretation and statistical conventions

Throughout this section:

  • ΔP = subsidiary praise rate minus headquarters praise rate.
  • ΔC = subsidiary complaint rate minus headquarters complaint rate.
  • ΔN = ΔP − ΔC.
  • All text differences are percentage points.
  • Positive ΔN favors subsidiaries.
  • Negative ΔN favors headquarters.
  • Intervals are the workbook's company-cluster bootstrap 95% confidence intervals.
  • The reported number of cells refers to matched company-job cells, not individual reviews.

An important qualification applies to the subgroup comparisons: the matching design remains within company and normalized title, but the composition of eligible company-job cells can differ between geographic groups.

Consequently, regional differences should be interpreted as descriptive heterogeneity, not automatically as causal effects of geographic institutions.

Seven geographic categories appear in the regional analysis, although the coded matched-cell results contain six reported regions: Asia, Europe, Latin America, Middle East, North America, and Oceania.

The corresponding star-rating analysis also includes Africa, illustrating an important difference in sample coverage between the two analyses.

The coded thematic analysis should therefore not be interpreted as exhausting all geographic regions represented in the underlying ratings dataset.

5.2. Asia: a pronounced managerial and organizational advantage

Asia represents the largest coded regional comparison.

  • Matched cells: 532.
  • Companies: 120.

Its relatively large sample provides a stronger basis for descriptive inference than the smaller regional groups.

The results reveal three simultaneous characteristics:

  1. A substantial benefits-praise disadvantage.
  2. Very large reductions in management and organizational complaints.
  3. A more favorable balance of working-time references.

5.2.1. Management and employee relations

The management domain exhibits:

  • HQ praise: 14.93%.
  • Subsidiary praise: 16.69%.
  • ΔP = +1.76 pp, 95% CI [0.15, 3.36].

Complaints:

  • HQ: 28.46%.
  • Subsidiaries: 19.49%.
  • ΔC = −8.97 pp, 95% CI [−10.34, −7.54].

The resulting net difference is:

ΔN = +10.73 pp, 95% CI [8.71, 12.94].

Furthermore, 71% of matched cells favor subsidiaries.

This is an unusually strong combination of evidence.

The result is:

  • Substantively large.
  • Statistically distinguishable from zero.
  • Supported by a large number of matching cells.
  • Present across a substantial majority of matched cells.
  • Reinforced by the star-rating results.

Unlike the overall management effect, which is almost entirely complaint-driven, the Asian effect combines:

  • More frequent positive managerial references.
  • Much less frequent negative managerial references.

Nevertheless, the reduction in complaints accounts for most of the net advantage.

Interpretation

The data are consistent with managerial experiences in Asian subsidiaries being comparatively favorable relative to those reported at their multinational headquarters.

However, at least three mechanisms could produce this result.

Mechanism A: Local managerial adaptation.

Subsidiaries may adjust management practices to local workplace expectations.

Mechanism B: Organizational buffering.

Subsidiary employees may encounter fewer corporate-level political and strategic tensions.

Mechanism C: Different reference groups.

Employees may compare multinational subsidiary management with locally available employment alternatives.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive.

The critical empirical question is whether the advantage survives controls for local labor-market context, reviewer composition, and differences in the organizational level being evaluated.

5.2.2. Organization and business

The organizational domain exhibits an even larger reduction in complaints.

Praise:

  • HQ: 22.19%.
  • Subsidiaries: 16.54%.
  • ΔP = −5.65 pp, 95% CI [−7.24, −4.07].

Complaints:

  • HQ: 34.62%.
  • Subsidiaries: 19.01%.
  • ΔC = −15.62 pp, 95% CI [−17.95, −13.42].

Net:

+9.96 pp, 95% CI [7.60, 12.33].

Again, 71% of matched cells favor subsidiaries.

The interpretation is particularly interesting.

Asian subsidiary employees discuss positive organizational characteristics considerably less frequently than employees at headquarters.

However, the reduction in negative organizational commentary is much larger.

This strongly suggests that the positive net outcome should not be equated with a generalized preference for the organization.

Instead, the key phenomenon is the substantially lower frequency of reported organizational problems.

One plausible explanation is different exposure to organizational decision-making and corporate restructuring.

Another is that headquarters employees are more informed about strategic problems and therefore more likely to identify them in reviews.

The data are compatible with both explanations.

5.2.3. Processes and coordination

This domain exhibits:

  • ΔP = +0.85 pp, 95% CI [0.22, 1.40].
  • ΔC = −5.24 pp, 95% CI [−6.33, −4.20].
  • ΔN = +6.09 pp, 95% CI [4.86, 7.17].

Furthermore, 69% of matched cells favor subsidiaries.

This result reinforces the organizational-complexity interpretation.

The improvement is not restricted to general management evaluations.

It extends to the processes through which work is coordinated.

The fact that Asian subsidiaries report substantially fewer coordination problems is theoretically consequential because subsidiaries are geographically separated from headquarters.

International business theory often highlights the coordination costs associated with geographic dispersion.

Yet these results may indicate that the burden of multinational coordination is not distributed equally across organizational locations.

A potentially important hypothesis is that headquarters employees absorb a disproportionate share of global integration and coordination demands.

However, this explanation requires direct testing using functions, managerial responsibilities, and organizational interfaces.

5.2.4. Benefits

The benefits domain moves in the opposite direction.

Praise:

  • HQ: 30.29%.
  • Subsidiaries: 18.57%.
  • ΔP = −11.72 pp, 95% CI [−13.58, −9.73].

Complaints:

  • HQ: 6.61%.
  • Subsidiaries: 4.73%.
  • ΔC = −1.88 pp, 95% CI [−2.68, −1.07].

Net:

−9.84 pp, 95% CI [−12.11, −7.33].

Only 30% of matched cells favor subsidiaries.

This indicates that the negative benefits difference is not confined to a small number of extreme observations.

The pattern is relatively widespread among matched cells.

Put differently, positive benefit references are substantially more common at headquarters even though negative benefit references are also somewhat more common there.

This is a particularly valuable result for a multidimensional employment-quality argument.

It suggests that relational and organizational experiences can favor subsidiaries while positive perceptions of employment benefits favor headquarters.

5.2.5. Culture and relationships

The culture domain shows:

  • ΔP = −1.08 pp, 95% CI [−2.94, 0.83].
  • ΔC = −5.14 pp, 95% CI [−6.95, −3.48].
  • ΔN = +4.05 pp, 95% CI [1.66, 6.96].

The effect is primarily complaint-driven.

The distinction relative to Europe is important.

In Asia, the culture-domain advantage is generated mainly by fewer negative references, rather than more praise.

This suggests that the same positive net cultural outcome may arise through different mechanisms across geographic contexts.

5.2.6. Working time

Asian subsidiaries also exhibit a positive working-time difference.

  • ΔP = +6.65 pp, 95% CI [4.40, 9.13].
  • ΔC = +2.19 pp, 95% CI [0.92, 3.50].
  • ΔN = +4.46 pp, 95% CI [1.65, 7.58].

This result requires a more nuanced interpretation.

Working-time arrangements are more frequently discussed positively in subsidiaries.

However, they are also discussed negatively more frequently.

The resulting net is positive because the difference in praise is larger.

This indicates greater thematic salience and a relatively more favorable balance, not an unambiguous reduction in working-time problems.

Interim assessment: Asia

The Asian findings are among the strongest in the workbook.

The most robust elements are:

  1. Lower management complaints.
  2. Lower organizational complaints.

  4. Lower benefits praise.
  5. Higher culture and management ratings.

This is a strong candidate for the central finding, provided that the pattern is not disproportionately driven by India or by a limited set of companies.

That qualification is important because India represents a large share of the Asian matched-cell observations.

5.3. Europe: stronger cultural praise, but weaker process and working-time outcomes

Europe contains:

  • 390 matched cells.
  • 80 companies.

The European pattern differs substantially from Asia's.

The strongest advantage concerns culture and relationships.

Management and organizational outcomes still favor subsidiaries, but by substantially smaller margins.

Meanwhile, processes and working time favor headquarters.

5.3.1. Culture and relationships

The European culture-domain result is:

  • ΔP = +6.24 pp, 95% CI [3.99, 8.67].
  • ΔC = −1.89 pp, 95% CI [−3.88, −0.01].
  • ΔN = +8.13 pp, 95% CI [5.08, 11.22].

The subsidiary advantage is supported by 65% of matched cells.

This is particularly important when compared with Asia.

In Asia, the cultural advantage largely results from fewer complaints.

In Europe, the advantage arises primarily from more praise.

This difference suggests qualitatively distinct cultural mechanisms.

A European subsidiary may generate a highly positive workplace atmosphere even if negative organizational experiences are not dramatically less frequent.

Alternatively, cultural differences in positive review-writing could contribute.

The distinction cannot be resolved using the aggregate domain outcome alone.

However, it provides a promising basis for country-specific coding validation.

5.3.2. Management and employee relations

The management domain shows:

  • ΔP = −0.81 pp, 95% CI [−2.26, 0.64].
  • ΔC = −3.37 pp, 95% CI [−5.10, −1.54].
  • ΔN = +2.56 pp, 95% CI [0.20, 4.98].

This advantage is substantially smaller than Asia's +10.73 pp.

The positive net result again comes from fewer complaints.

However, only 55% of European matched cells favor subsidiaries.

The evidence therefore indicates a relatively modest relational advantage compared with Asia.

5.3.3. Organization and business

The organizational domain shows:

  • ΔP = −0.77 pp.
  • ΔC = −5.55 pp, 95% CI [−7.63, −3.88].
  • ΔN = +4.78 pp, 95% CI [2.60, 7.10].

The result is complaint-driven.

As in Asia, headquarters employees report more organizational problems.

However, the gap is smaller.

This may reflect national institutional environments, organizational composition, or differences in the roles performed by the matched employees.

Formal international interaction tests are required before assigning the difference to any particular institutional mechanism.

5.3.4. Processes and coordination: a reversal

Europe shows:

  • ΔP = −0.46 pp.
  • ΔC = +2.33 pp, 95% CI [0.11, 4.37].
  • ΔN = −2.79 pp, 95% CI [−4.81, −0.61].

This reverses the Asian result.

Asia's net process advantage is +6.09 pp.

Europe's net process difference is −2.79 pp.

The contrast in point estimates is:

8.88 percentage points.

This is one of the most interesting geographic interactions in the dataset.

It suggests that the extent to which foreign subsidiaries experience favorable coordination processes may depend on regional operational and institutional context.

However, the contrast does not by itself constitute a formal statistical test of an Asia-Europe interaction.

A clustered interaction model is needed.

Theoretical significance

One interpretation is that some subsidiaries benefit from organizational separation, whereas others experience additional coordination demands through their relationships with headquarters.

This suggests that geographic distance and organizational centrality may interact with regional governance arrangements.

It also challenges any claim that foreign subsidiaries are universally less bureaucratic.

5.3.5. Working time: another reversal

Europe shows:

  • ΔP = −2.34 pp, 95% CI [−4.16, −0.77].
  • ΔC = +0.66 pp.
  • ΔN = −2.99 pp, 95% CI [−6.15, −0.10].

The effect favors headquarters.

Asia, by contrast, exhibits a positive net difference of +4.46 pp.

Thus, the descriptive Asia-Europe contrast equals 7.45 pp.

This result may reflect differences in:

  • Local working-time expectations.
  • Job composition.
  • Coordination across time zones.
  • Employment regulation.
  • The organizational distribution of global responsibilities.

The evidence does not yet identify which explanation is most important.

5.3.6. Benefits

It would therefore be inappropriate to describe the European benefits net gap as statistically established at the nominal 5% level.

The European evidence suggests that the general headquarters advantage in benefits is not uniformly strong across host regions.

5.8. What the regional analysis tells us

The regional analysis produces five major insights.

Insight 1. The benefits advantage is geographically concentrated

The headquarters benefits advantage is particularly strong in Asia.

It is much weaker and statistically inconclusive in Europe and North America.

This suggests that the benefits difference should not be interpreted as a universal feature of multinational headquarters.

Insight 2. Cultural advantages arise through different channels

Asia:

  • More favorable net culture primarily through fewer complaints.

Europe:

  • More favorable net culture primarily through more praise.

North America:

  • More favorable net culture primarily through more praise.

This suggests that similar aggregate outcomes may emerge from different mechanisms.

Insight 3. Management advantages are highly uneven geographically

Asia has a major positive management effect.

Europe has a relatively modest effect.

Oceania exhibits a negative effect.

This strongly motivates a geographic moderation analysis.

Insight 4. Coordination is the most geographically polarized domain

Processes and coordination exhibit:

  • Asia: +6.09 pp.
  • Europe: −2.79 pp.
  • Middle East: −7.00 pp.
  • Oceania: −3.67 pp.
  • North America: −0.09 pp.

For a theory of multinational organization, this is especially important.

It suggests that geographically dispersed work may generate different coordination experiences depending on organizational and local context.

Insight 5. The strongest geographic pattern is multidimensional

No single domain adequately represents subsidiary employment quality.

Asia illustrates this particularly clearly:

  • Favorable management.
  • Favorable organizational functioning.
  • Favorable processes.
  • Unfavorable benefits praise.

Europe presents another combination:

  • Favorable culture.
  • Moderately favorable management.
  • Unfavorable coordination.
  • Unfavorable working time.

The emerging theoretical challenge is explaining how location changes the composition of employment advantages, rather than simply whether one location is better.

7. The domestic-versus-international border test

7.1. Why this may be the most important analysis in the workbook

The regional and country comparisons establish substantial differences between headquarters and foreign subsidiaries.

However, they do not answer an essential theoretical question:

Are these differences caused by crossing national borders, or do they simply reflect differences between headquarters and other establishments of the same firm?

This distinction is fundamental.

Many features of headquarters employment may differ from employment elsewhere in the same country.

For example, headquarters employees might:

  • Participate more directly in strategic decision-making.
  • Encounter greater organizational complexity.
  • Have greater exposure to corporate restructuring.
  • Interact more frequently with senior executives.
  • Experience different career opportunities.

If these organizational differences explain the main results, one might expect similar patterns when comparing headquarters employees with employees in other domestic locations.

Conversely, if international institutional environments or cross-national employment practices matter, foreign establishments should exhibit additional differences.

The workbook includes a particularly valuable comparison for US-headquartered firms.

The reference location is the headquarters state.

Two categories are then compared with that reference:

  1. Employees working in another US state.
  2. Employees working abroad.

This design does not perfectly isolate the causal effect of crossing an international border.

However, it offers a much stronger descriptive test than comparing headquarters and foreign subsidiaries alone.

7.12. Theoretical assessment of the border test

The border test substantially changes how the study should be positioned.

Without it, the main result could be interpreted as:

Employees at corporate headquarters experience more organizational problems than employees elsewhere in the firm.

With the border test, a more distinctive possibility emerges:

The organizational and relational advantages associated with foreign subsidiary employment are considerably more pronounced than the corresponding differences between headquarters and other domestic establishments.

This shifts the theoretical question.

The study is no longer solely about the organizational consequences of headquarters centrality.

It becomes a study of how international boundaries and organizational location jointly shape reported employment experiences within multinational enterprises.

That is potentially a stronger international business contribution.

However, the finding should be described as a candidate border-specific association, not a demonstrated causal border effect.

To establish a stronger inference, the analysis would need:

  1. Comparable company-job samples on both sides of the domestic-international contrast.
  2. Direct statistical tests of foreign-versus-domestic interactions.
  3. Controls for sector, function, review period, and employment composition.
  4. Country-level institutional measures.
  5. Sensitivity tests excluding India.
  6. Consistent results after addressing review-writing and coding differences.

8. Interim ranking of the geographic findings

After examining the regions, countries, and border test, I would provisionally rank the results as follows.

Rank 1. A foreign-specific relational and organizational advantage

Evidence supporting it

  • Strong overall management and organization gaps.
  • Large Asian and Indian effects.
  • Positive culture and management ratings.
  • Stronger foreign than domestic headquarters comparisons.
  • Substantially higher foreign overall ratings.

Evidence against or qualifications

  • Management advantages are smaller in Europe.
  • Oceania exhibits a negative management estimate.
  • Some country samples are small.
  • Foreign and domestic comparisons have different eligible matched cells.
  • Reporting and information-exposure differences remain plausible.

Assessment: Strongest candidate for a central international business contribution.

Rank 2. An international benefits-praise disadvantage

Evidence supporting it

  • Strong negative overall benefits net difference.
  • Large Asian and Indian differences.
  • Negative effects across multiple individual benefits themes.
  • Domestic benefits net difference reverses direction relative to the foreign comparison.

Evidence against or qualifications

  • Benefits complaints are also less frequent abroad.
  • European and North American net differences are weaker.
  • Compensation-and-benefits ratings favor subsidiaries overall.
  • National differences in social protection may influence salience.
  • Objective benefit generosity is not measured.

Assessment: Strong and theoretically important, but its interpretation as an objective employment disadvantage remains conditional.

Rank 3. Geographic recombination of working conditions

Evidence supporting it

  • Asia combines strongly favorable management with unfavorable benefits praise.
  • Europe combines favorable culture with unfavorable coordination.
  • Singapore combines favorable management with unfavorable workload.
  • Ireland combines favorable organization with unfavorable working time.

Evidence against or qualifications

  • Several interactions rely on small country samples.
  • Cross-domain configurations do not establish employee-level tradeoffs.
  • Company and job composition differ across geographic categories.
  • Multiple testing remains unadjusted.

Assessment: Potentially the most novel theoretical contribution, but requires additional formal testing.

Rank 4. Headquarters exposure to organizational complexity

Evidence supporting it

  • More headquarters complaints about organizational change.
  • More complaints concerning job security.
  • More complaints about corporate strategy and scale.
  • Greater headquarters complaints about bureaucracy overall.
  • Particularly strong organizational differences in Asia.

Evidence against or qualifications

  • Domestic establishments do not reproduce the foreign organizational pattern.
  • European and Middle Eastern subsidiaries report less favorable process experiences.
  • Information access may affect theme frequency.
  • The data do not directly measure coordination responsibility or organizational centrality.

Assessment: Promising explanatory mechanism, but probably insufficient as a complete theory.

Rank 5. Distinct career advantages associated with different organizational locations

Evidence supporting it

  • Learning through work favors subsidiaries.
  • Internal mobility favors headquarters.
  • Career Opportunities star ratings favor subsidiaries.
  • Several occupational and geographic subgroups differ substantially.

Evidence against or qualifications

  • The aggregate learning-and-career domain gap is inconclusive.
  • Skill development and subsequent promotion are not directly observed.
  • Title equivalence is particularly important here.
  • Career ratings use a broader construct than individual coded themes.

Assessment: A potentially valuable secondary contribution.

9. What the geographic evidence contributes to the research question

The geographic evidence suggests that the distinction between headquarters and foreign subsidiaries is meaningful, but it is not adequately represented by a single employment-quality ranking.

The strongest overall result involves more favorable reported management, organizational, and cultural experiences abroad.

However, the nature of those advantages differs geographically.

In Asia, the relative organizational benefit is especially pronounced and largely complaint-driven.

In Europe, the clearest advantage concerns positive cultural evaluations.

In North America, the differences are generally more limited.

In some smaller country samples, subsidiaries combine favorable managerial evaluations with unfavorable workload or working-time experiences.

Most importantly, the domestic-versus-international border test suggests that the foreign-location advantage is not simply a generic feature of employment outside the physical headquarters.

That result makes an explicitly international business explanation both more necessary and more promising.

The next analytical priority is to determine whether this geographic pattern survives differences in:

  • Industry.
  • Occupational function.
  • Job level.
  • Employment status.
  • Historical period.
  • Employee tenure.
  • Current or former employment.
  • Individual multinational.

These dimensions may establish whether the phenomenon is broadly organizational, concentrated in particular labor markets, or driven by specific groups of employees.

10.5. Implications for international business theory

This headquarters-origin imbalance matters for theoretical interpretation.

The study may ultimately identify a phenomenon associated with:

  1. Multinational hierarchy generally.
  2. US multinational management practices.
  3. Differences between US headquarters employment and foreign labor markets.
  4. A combination of these processes.

These alternatives cannot currently be separated.

An important theoretical recommendation follows:

The initial contribution should be framed explicitly around multinational employment differentiation, while acknowledging that its empirical foundation is predominantly US-headquartered firms.

A universal theory of all multinational headquarters would currently overstate the evidence.

15. A potentially important occupational discovery

The results suggest that there may be two different forms of foreign subsidiary employment experience.

15.1. Professional and managerial subsidiary employment

This configuration is characterized by:

  • Fewer management complaints.
  • Fewer organizational complaints.
  • More favorable cultural narratives.
  • Lower benefits praise.
  • Generally inconclusive workload net differences.

It is particularly visible among:

  • General management.
  • Software engineering.
  • Other engineering.
  • Data and analytics.
  • Professional employees.

15.2. Frontline and operational subsidiary employment

This configuration is characterized by:

  • Potentially more favorable workplace culture.
  • Less favorable workload.
  • Less favorable working-time arrangements in some functions.
  • Less favorable compensation narratives in some operational roles.
  • Weaker evidence of a broad management or organizational advantage.

It is particularly visible among:

  • Store and restaurant employees.
  • Warehouse and manufacturing employees.
  • Frontline workers more generally.

15.3. Why this distinction matters

This is potentially a more theoretically meaningful distinction than job level alone.

A multinational may generate different employment experiences for employees performing:

  1. Knowledge-intensive professional work.
  2. Managerial and coordination work.
  3. Customer-facing operational work.
  4. Routine physical or service work.

The location of the job within the multinational may affect each category differently.

However, this remains an interpretation of separate subgroup estimates.

It is not yet evidence of a statistically established interaction between foreign location and occupational task characteristics.

The next empirical step should involve formal models with:

  • Company fixed effects.
  • Normalized job-title controls.
  • Destination-country controls.
  • Occupational function interactions.
  • Job-level interactions.
  • Review-period controls.

The analysis should also examine whether the same firms contribute to the professional and operational results.

Otherwise, observed differences may reflect differences between companies rather than within-company occupational heterogeneity.

17. Preliminary theoretical implications of occupational heterogeneity

The organizational and occupational results help refine the candidate contribution.

A simple claim that foreign subsidiaries offer better employment experiences is insufficient.

The evidence instead suggests that foreign location changes the configuration of employment advantages, and this configuration differs by employee function and organizational level.

Three theoretical ideas are emerging.

17.1. Organizational centrality may impose hidden employment costs

Headquarters employees may benefit from:

  • Access to strategic work.
  • Professional networks.
  • Internal mobility.
  • Benefits.
  • Organizational resources.

Yet they may also encounter:

  • Organizational restructuring.
  • Strategic uncertainty.
  • Internal politics.
  • More complex coordination.
  • Greater exposure to corporate decision-making.

The results are consistent with this interpretation, particularly for professionals and managers.

However, the domestic border comparison indicates that organizational centrality alone may not explain the full international result.

17.2. Local adaptation may improve relational experiences without improving all material conditions

The strong cultural and managerial results are compatible with differences in local management practices.

However, compensation, benefits, and workload do not consistently favor subsidiaries.

Therefore, local adaptation may affect employment domains differently.

This could help explain why multinational organizational integration does not produce identical employee experiences.

17.3. Employment quality may be occupationally segmented within the multinational

Professional and managerial workers exhibit strong organizational advantages.

Operational workers frequently show less favorable workload or working-time balances.

This raises the possibility of an internal segmentation of multinational employment experiences.

However, occupational segmentation should not be inferred solely from subgroup averages.

It needs direct testing within comparable companies, countries, and matched titles.

19. Interim conclusion

The organizational and occupational analysis strengthens the central finding but also imposes important boundaries.

The management and organizational advantages associated with foreign subsidiaries are visible across multiple industries and professional functions.

They are not confined to one sector or one hierarchy level.

However, the occupational results reveal a different picture for frontline and operational work.

Some operational employees report more favorable workplace culture while encountering less favorable workload and working-time conditions.

This suggests that the multinational headquarters-subsidiary relationship is not simply a question of which location provides better employment.

It concerns how organizational location, occupational role, and national context jointly shape different dimensions of the employment experience.

Three findings now deserve particular empirical priority:

  1. The foreign-specific management and organizational advantage, especially the reduction in complaints.
  2. The benefits-praise disadvantage, which appears concentrated in foreign rather than domestic establishments.
  3. The occupational division between relational experiences and work demands, particularly among professional versus frontline employees.

The next part will assess whether these patterns vary across time, employment status, tenure, and current versus former employees.

These dimensions are essential because they may reveal whether the observed advantages reflect stable organizational conditions or differences in reviewer composition, employee career stage, and employment transitions.

PART IV. TEMPORAL, EMPLOYMENT-STATUS, AND TENURE HETEROGENEITY

20. Does the headquarters-subsidiary difference change over time?

The preceding analyses established three important findings.

First, foreign subsidiaries exhibit more favorable managerial and organizational narratives than headquarters, particularly among professional and managerial employees.

Second, benefits praise is consistently more prominent at headquarters, whereas compensation differences vary substantially by sector and function.

Third, operational employees sometimes report less favorable workload and working-time conditions despite positive cultural evaluations.

We now examine whether these relationships depend on the period in which employees wrote their reviews, the nature of their employment, their tenure, and whether they remain employed by the company.

These dimensions are important for both theory and research validity.

If the observed differences reflect relatively stable multinational organizational structures, we might expect them to persist across periods and employee groups.

Conversely, if they primarily reflect temporary economic conditions, review selection, or individual employment transitions, the gaps may fluctuate substantially.

The central discovery in this part is that the managerial, organizational, and benefits asymmetries persist across periods, but the overall rating advantage appears smaller in more recent years. Moreover, the subsidiary advantage becomes particularly pronounced among longer-tenured employees.

The latter finding is potentially important, but it must be considered alongside survivor selection.

31. Tenure: does the subsidiary advantage grow with employment duration?

Tenure may be one of the most theoretically important dimensions in the workbook.

A multinational's organizational environment may be understood differently by newly hired employees and long-tenured employees.

New employees may evaluate their workplace through:

  • Recruitment expectations.
  • Initial training.
  • Early workplace relationships.
  • Adjustment to organizational procedures.

Long-tenured employees may place greater emphasis on:

  • Career mobility.
  • Organizational stability.
  • Management quality.
  • Internal politics.
  • Benefits accumulation.
  • Long-term employment expectations.

Consequently, tenure can reveal whether the headquarters-subsidiary difference is concentrated among employees at particular stages of organizational membership.

However, tenure is also vulnerable to selection bias.

Employees who remain in a company for many years are not a random sample of those originally hired.

This is particularly important when comparing headquarters and subsidiaries.

33.2. Possible theoretical interpretation

One explanation is that longer-tenured headquarters employees accumulate greater exposure to:

  • Organizational politics.
  • Leadership changes.
  • Corporate restructuring.
  • Promotion decisions.
  • Management conflicts.
  • Strategic uncertainty.

However, the current design cannot establish that interpretation.

33.3. Survival selection

A crucial alternative explanation is differential employee retention.

Suppose dissatisfied subsidiary employees leave earlier than dissatisfied headquarters employees.

In that case, long-tenured subsidiary reviewers would be positively selected.

The observed management gap could increase with tenure even without cumulative deterioration in headquarters conditions.

Conversely, headquarters employees may remain despite dissatisfaction because of stronger benefits, promotion opportunities, or other employment advantages.

This could produce a similar pattern.

Therefore, the tenure gradient may reflect:

  1. Cumulative exposure.
  2. Selective retention.
  3. Cohort differences.
  4. Differences in the composition of jobs surviving into higher tenure categories.
  5. Changes in review-writing behavior with tenure.

These explanations must be distinguished before developing a causal theory.

47. The most important unresolved issue: are we observing conditions or reporting behavior?

The cumulative analysis has revealed a recurring feature.

Many of the largest positive net differences arise because headquarters employees mention particular problems more frequently.

For example:

  • Management complaints.
  • Organizational change complaints.
  • Leadership communication complaints.
  • Job-security complaints.
  • Bureaucracy complaints.

Conversely, many of the strongest headquarters advantages arise because benefits are mentioned positively more frequently.

This creates a fundamental interpretive challenge.

Are subsidiaries objectively providing better managerial experiences and less generous benefits?

Or do headquarters and subsidiary employees differ in what they notice, consider important, and choose to mention?

The data cannot yet distinguish these mechanisms.

Importantly, however, a generalized review-positivity explanation is insufficient to account for every observed pattern.

The evidence shows:

  • Positive cultural mentions are greater in some regions but not others.
  • Management advantages are often driven by fewer complaints rather than more praise.
  • Benefits praise is lower abroad even when compensation ratings are higher.
  • Operational employees sometimes report greater workload complaints abroad.
  • Some domains reverse between regions, functions, and employment statuses.

Therefore, any reporting-style explanation would need to account for domain-specific and context-dependent variation.

That is possible, but it is more complex than a simple tendency for foreign subsidiary employees to write more favorable reviews.

This observation provides a useful foundation for the measurement-validity and alternative-explanations section.

48. Interim conclusions from the temporal and employment analyses

The analysis now supports five provisional conclusions.

1. Foreign subsidiary advantages are not limited to a particular historical period

The managerial and organizational patterns appear across the observation period.

Their magnitudes fluctuate, but the main positive differences persist.

2. The benefits-praise disadvantage is similarly persistent

The negative benefits result is visible across periods and among both current and former employees.

It is especially pronounced among longer-tenured workers.

3. Occupational and employment-status differences matter substantially

Regular and professional employees show relatively strong organizational advantages.

Part-time and operational employees exhibit more mixed employment configurations, often involving less favorable compensation or workload.

4. Tenure reveals potentially important cumulative differences

Longer-tenured employees report substantially larger subsidiary rating advantages.

The corresponding management and benefits differences also become more pronounced.

The point estimates suggest a smaller subsidiary rating premium in recent years.

50. Implications for the remainder of the report

At this stage, the major subgroup dimensions have been examined.

However, two important tasks remain before developing the theoretical argument.

First, the identified interactions need to be connected to individual themes.

This includes examining whether:

  • India's organizational advantage concentrates in particular themes.
  • European differences reflect specific kinds of coordination problems.
  • Professional and operational occupations differ on particular workload complaints.
  • The tenure gradient is driven by organizational change, management quality, or other specific themes.

Second, we need to evaluate company-level heterogeneity.

The company-spread sheet may reveal whether the overall advantages are:

  • Broadly distributed across multinationals.
  • Concentrated in particular firms.
  • Strongly polarized between companies.
  • Associated with extreme positive or negative company-level differences.

This distinction is crucial for theoretical interpretation.

A pattern driven by most firms would support a relatively general theory of multinational employment differentiation.

A pattern driven by a small number of companies would suggest a more conditional explanation emphasizing firm-specific governance and HR practices.

The next part will therefore investigate the theme-level interactions and company spread before making a final determination about the strongest theoretical contribution.

PART V. THEME-LEVEL INTERACTIONS, COMPANY HETEROGENEITY, AND THE SEARCH FOR THE CENTRAL DISCOVERY

51. Why the theme-level interactions matter

The earlier parts established that the headquarters-subsidiary differences are not uniform across regions, occupations, employment statuses, or tenure.

However, an important question remains:

Are the subgroup differences driven by the same underlying workplace experiences, or do different themes account for the apparent subsidiary advantages in different contexts?

This distinction is theoretically consequential.

For example, two countries may exhibit similar positive management-domain differences while differing substantially in their underlying causes.

In one country, the difference might reflect fewer complaints about immediate supervisors.

In another, it might reflect fewer complaints about organizational leadership or employee treatment.

Similarly, a favorable culture-domain difference could coexist with an unfavorable result for specific coworker relationships.

A theoretical model based only on broad domains might therefore conceal important mechanisms.

The theme-level results allow us to examine these distinctions more precisely.

The second objective of this part is to determine whether the aggregate results are broadly distributed across companies or disproportionately driven by particular multinationals.

This distinction is especially important because the study seeks to contribute to international business theory.

A finding that repeatedly appears within many multinationals has different theoretical implications from one concentrated in a small number of unusual firms.

68.1. Does NO_CONS explain the subsidiary advantage?

It could contribute.

Suppose subsidiary reviewers are more likely to write a short statement indicating that they have no complaints.

Such statements enter the complaint-text denominator but contain few substantive negative themes.

This could mechanically reduce the observed frequency of other complaints.

Consequently, some of the apparent subsidiary advantage might arise from differences in complaint-text composition rather than actual employment conditions.

However, the available results do not establish that this mechanism fully explains the findings.

For example:

  • The managerial and organizational advantages persist across several countries.
  • The UK exhibits a smaller no-cons difference but still has positive organizational and cultural results.
  • Former and current employees both exhibit favorable subsidiary management differences.
  • Independent star ratings favor subsidiaries.

The appropriate conclusion is that NO_CONS is a serious measurement concern requiring direct correction.

68.2. Recommended correction

The analysis should be repeated using at least three specifications.

Specification 1: Original denominator

Retain the existing measures.

Specification 2: Exclude no-cons texts

Remove explicit no-cons statements from the complaint denominator and re-estimate substantive complaint rates.

Specification 3: Two-part model

Model separately:

  1. The probability that a reviewer reports no substantive complaints.
  2. The probability that a substantive complaint mentions each workplace theme.

This distinction is important.

A no-cons statement may itself be a meaningful employee response.

However, it should not be treated as an ordinary complaint.

The two-part model would separate the reporting decision from the themes discussed conditional on substantive reporting.

68.3. Why this test has high priority

If the management and organizational advantages remain large after correcting for no-cons statements, the substantive interpretation becomes considerably stronger.

If they shrink substantially, the central finding would need to be reframed as a mixture of employment experiences and review-writing behavior.

Either result would improve the study.

77.1. A methodological qualification

The company-level estimates do not establish that all companies contribute equally to the overall results.

The overall analysis weights matched cells equally.

The company-spread analysis summarizes company-level mean gaps among firms with at least five matched cells.

These are different weighting schemes and samples.

Therefore, the apparent consistency across companies should be tested formally by:

  1. Re-estimating the main results with equal company weights.
  2. Comparing the resulting estimates with equal-cell estimates.
  3. Performing leave-one-company-out analyses.
  4. Estimating the distribution of company-specific effects.
  5. Examining whether results vary with the number of matched cells per company.

These tests would establish whether the central finding is robust to influential firms and weighting choices.

78. A critical methodological issue: the joint distribution of company outcomes

The company-spread sheet contains domain-specific company-level differences.

This creates an opportunity to investigate whether the principal employment advantages are correlated at company level.

For example:

  • Do companies with stronger subsidiary management advantages also exhibit stronger subsidiary culture advantages?
  • Do companies with large headquarters benefits advantages also have large subsidiary management advantages?
  • Are organizational and coordination differences strongly correlated?
  • Do companies with workload disadvantages exhibit smaller overall rating advantages?

These questions are essential for evaluating a possible employment-quality tradeoff.

However, simply observing positive management gaps and negative benefits gaps in the aggregate does not establish that the two are systematically related across firms.

A genuine tradeoff hypothesis requires evidence of a negative association between those advantages.

Even a negative company-level correlation would not establish a causal exchange.

It might reflect differences in countries, industries, and job composition.

The company-spread data therefore provide a useful starting point, but not a complete test.

79. Reassessing the importance of the central discovery

The results now provide evidence at four levels.

Level 1. Overall matched-cell differences

Management, organization, and culture favor subsidiaries.

Benefits favor headquarters.

Level 2. Geographic heterogeneity

The magnitude and composition of these differences vary across regions and countries.

Level 3. Occupational heterogeneity

Professional and managerial employees show strong organizational advantages.

Operational employees sometimes report less favorable work demands.

Level 4. Company-level prevalence

Management and organization favor subsidiaries in a substantial majority of eligible companies.

This fourth level is especially important.

It suggests that the central managerial and organizational findings are not merely artifacts of pooling a few highly influential firms.

However, the interpretation remains subject to measurement validity, company selection, and formal robustness testing.

80. The candidate findings after the full descriptive investigation

At this stage, I would distinguish between findings that are descriptively established, findings that remain conditional, and findings requiring additional analysis.

80.1. Strongest descriptive finding: the foreign managerial-organizational advantage

The evidence is unusually consistent.

Overall:

  • Management: +6.75 pp.
  • Organization: +6.98 pp.

Company prevalence:

  • 54 of 61 eligible companies favor subsidiaries on management.
  • 50 of 61 favor subsidiaries on organization.

  • Senior Management: +0.208 points.
  • Culture & Values: +0.256 points.

Positive differences persist across historical periods and among current and former employees.

Assessment

This is the most defensible central empirical contribution.

The strongest interpretation is:

Foreign subsidiary employees report substantially fewer managerial and organizational problems than comparable headquarters employees, and this difference is broadly distributed across firms and occupational contexts.

The statement should remain focused on reported experiences until measurement and selection threats are addressed.

81. Second candidate: relational advantages coexist with material disadvantages

The second major finding concerns the contrasting directions of employment domains.

The core pattern is:

  • Favorable culture.
  • Favorable management.
  • Favorable organizational experience.
  • Unfavorable benefits praise.

This pattern appears across many subgroups.

However, compensation is more heterogeneous than benefits.

Therefore, it would be inaccurate to describe the finding as a general material-reward disadvantage abroad.

A more precise formulation is:

Foreign subsidiaries exhibit more favorable reported relational and organizational experiences, while headquarters employees more frequently praise nonwage benefits.

This is a stronger and more defensible statement than claiming that subsidiaries provide worse total compensation.

Theoretical significance

The finding suggests that different elements of the employment relationship may be shaped by different international and organizational mechanisms.

For example:

  • Benefits may depend strongly on national institutional arrangements.
  • Management experiences may depend on local adaptation and organizational structure.
  • Organizational complaints may reflect proximity to corporate decision-making.
  • Compensation may depend on occupation-specific labor-market conditions.

This differentiated explanation has greater theoretical potential than a single headquarters-subsidiary employment-quality hierarchy.

This is potentially the most novel extension of the main finding.

Tenure-contingent differentiation of multinational employment experiences.

83. Fourth candidate: occupational segmentation of headquarters-subsidiary experiences

The occupational results suggest different employment configurations.

Professional and managerial functions exhibit relatively favorable organizational experiences abroad.

Operational and frontline functions sometimes exhibit less favorable:

  • Workload.
  • Working time.
  • Compensation.

This produces the possibility of occupationally segmented employment experiences within multinational firms.

However, the evidence is partly based on small samples.

Moreover, occupations differ across companies, sectors, and countries.

A formal within-company interaction test is needed.

Assessment

Promising as a boundary condition.

Potentially important for international HRM.

Not yet strong enough to displace the main managerial-organizational finding.

84. Fifth candidate: international boundaries matter beyond headquarters centrality

The border test is particularly theoretically important.

The foreign comparison shows much larger culture, management, and organizational advantages than the comparison with other US states.

The benefits pattern also reverses direction between domestic and foreign establishments.

This suggests that the headquarters-subsidiary asymmetry is not simply a generic consequence of geographic separation from corporate headquarters.

However, the samples differ, and the contrasts are not formally tested.

Assessment

This may ultimately become the most important theoretical component.

It distinguishes the study from more general organizational research on corporate headquarters.

A rigorous border-specific interaction analysis could transform the paper from a descriptive study of organizational location into a more distinctive contribution to international business theory.

85. Recommended central empirical narrative

Having examined the overall results, every major subgroup dimension, selected theme-level interactions, and company spread, I would recommend the following central framing.

Proposed central finding

Multinational headquarters and foreign subsidiaries offer systematically different reported employment experiences, even within matched company-job comparisons. Foreign subsidiary employees report fewer managerial and organizational problems and often more favorable workplace culture, while headquarters employees report stronger positive benefits experiences. These differences are substantially more pronounced across international borders than across domestic establishments, and their composition varies by country, occupation, and tenure.

This formulation has several strengths.

First, it is directly supported by the strongest descriptive findings.

Second, it does not incorrectly claim that all subsidiary working conditions are superior.

Third, it separates reported experience from objectively measured working conditions.

Fourth, it incorporates the international-border comparison.

Fifth, it recognizes important geographic and occupational boundary conditions.

Sixth, it provides a foundation for a theoretical explanation involving both organizational and institutional mechanisms.

86. What should not become the central claim

Several tempting interpretations should be avoided.

Claim 1: Foreign subsidiaries provide better working conditions overall

Too broad.

Compensation, benefits, workload, and working time do not consistently favor subsidiaries.

Claim 2: Headquarters employees are more dissatisfied

Not directly established by the text measures.

The overall ratings favor subsidiaries, but thematic mentions are not equivalent to satisfaction.

Claim 3: Subsidiary managers are objectively better

Not established.

The evidence concerns reported managerial experiences, not independently assessed management quality.

Claim 4: Headquarters pay more

Not established.

Pay-level and compensation results are mixed.

Claim 5: Foreign employees trade lower benefits for better culture

Potentially interesting, but not yet demonstrated.

The observed differences do not establish an intentional or causal tradeoff.

Claim 6: Subsidiary employment improves with tenure

Not established.

The tenure gradient may reflect survivor selection.

Claim 7: Country institutions explain the geographic differences

Plausible, but not yet tested.

Country-level patterns are descriptive associations.

87. A potentially stronger theoretical interpretation

The strongest theoretical contribution may not be a simple headquarters-versus-subsidiary comparison.

Instead, it may concern the differentiated international organization of employment experiences.

Multinationals distribute across locations not only production, services, and decision-making activities, but also different:

  • Organizational responsibilities.
  • Managerial relationships.
  • Employment rewards.
  • Career opportunities.
  • Work demands.
  • Exposure to corporate uncertainty.

These dimensions may not be distributed uniformly.

The location of an employee within the multinational may therefore shape the bundle of employment experiences available to that employee.

Importantly, the bundle may differ by occupation and institutional environment.

This perspective would connect international business research with international HRM and organizational theory.

It would also allow the study to explain why foreign subsidiary employment can be evaluated more favorably overall despite disadvantages in particular employment domains.

The contribution would be strongest if framed as a theory of multidimensional employment differentiation, rather than as a universal subsidiary advantage.

88. Interim conclusion

The full descriptive analysis points toward one especially robust finding and several potentially important extensions.

The strongest finding is the widespread relative absence of negative managerial and organizational narratives in foreign subsidiaries.

It appears:

  • In the overall matched sample.
  • Across multiple sectors.
  • Across major professional and managerial functions.
  • Among current and former employees.
  • Across historical periods.
  • In a large majority of eligible companies.
  • Alongside higher culture and senior-management ratings.

The benefits-praise disadvantage provides the most important contrasting dimension.

Geography, occupation, and tenure then reveal substantial boundary conditions.

The central theoretical challenge is to explain why a multinational's organizational and employment practices are experienced differently across locations despite shared corporate ownership and nominally matched jobs.

The next step is to connect these patterns to established theories rather than develop an explanation solely from the descriptive correlations.

That connection requires careful consideration of:

  1. Headquarters centrality and multinational organizational structure.
  2. Subsidiary autonomy and local adaptation.
  3. National institutional differences.
  4. Firm-specific labor-market advantages.
  5. Employee reference groups and organizational identification.
  6. Employment selection and tenure.
  7. The distinction between material and relational employment quality.

The theoretical discussion must also recognize that several mechanisms predict competing outcomes.

That tension is an opportunity for contribution.

PART VI. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND AND PROPOSED CONTRIBUTIONS

89. From empirical regularities to theoretical explanation

The preceding analysis identified a potentially important phenomenon in multinational employment:

Employees working in foreign subsidiaries report more favorable managerial, organizational, and often cultural experiences than employees at headquarters, while headquarters employees report stronger positive experiences concerning benefits.

The central pattern is particularly interesting for international business research because:

  1. Employees are compared within the same multinational.
  2. Comparisons are matched by normalized job title.
  3. The managerial and organizational differences are relatively widespread across companies.
  4. The differences are substantially more pronounced internationally than across domestic establishments.
  5. The effects vary across host countries, occupational functions, and tenure.
  6. The overall ratings favor subsidiaries, even when specific employment dimensions favor headquarters.

The theoretical challenge is to explain why common corporate ownership and organizational identity coexist with systematically different reported employment experiences.

A satisfactory explanation must address three questions.

First: Why might headquarters employees experience or report more organizational and managerial problems?

Second: Why might foreign subsidiary employees report stronger cultural and relational experiences without consistently enjoying better compensation, benefits, or workload conditions?

Third: Why do these relationships vary across institutional environments, occupations, and employee tenure?

No single established theory fully answers all three questions.

The most promising approach is to combine selected insights from international business, international human resource management, organizational theory, labor economics, and social psychology.

However, theoretical integration should be disciplined.

The purpose is not to assemble a long list of potentially relevant theories.

It is to identify a small number of mechanisms that generate distinct, testable predictions corresponding to the observed results.

90. International business theory: multinational integration and local differentiation

90.1. The integration-responsiveness framework

One of the most established approaches to understanding multinational organization concerns the tension between global integration and local responsiveness.

Foundational references

  • Prahalad, C. K., & Doz, Y. L. (1987). The Multinational Mission: Balancing Local Demands and Global Vision. Free Press.
  • Bartlett, C. A., & Ghoshal, S. (1989). Managing Across Borders: The Transnational Solution. Harvard Business School Press.

The central insight is that multinational enterprises face competing pressures.

On one hand, they seek integration across countries to achieve:

  • Economies of scale.
  • Consistent organizational systems.
  • Coordinated strategies.
  • Shared knowledge.
  • Common corporate standards.

On the other hand, subsidiaries must respond to local conditions, including:

  • Labor-market institutions.
  • Cultural expectations.
  • Customer requirements.
  • Employment regulation.
  • Competitive conditions.

This tension has direct implications for employment experiences.

A multinational may establish common HR policies while allowing local subsidiaries to adapt their everyday implementation.

Thus, employees may formally belong to the same corporation but experience different managerial practices and workplace arrangements.

Application to the findings

The framework is particularly relevant to three results.

First, culture and management differences.

Foreign subsidiaries may adapt workplace practices to local expectations, producing different employee experiences despite a common corporate identity.

Second, benefits differences.

Benefits may be more directly shaped by national employment institutions and locally adapted HR arrangements.

Third, geographic heterogeneity.

The advantages observed in Asia, Europe, and North America may differ because the pressures for localization vary across host environments.

Theoretical limitation

The integration-responsiveness framework does not inherently predict that subsidiaries will have better management.

Local responsiveness can generate favorable or unfavorable outcomes.

It explains why experiences might differ, but not necessarily the direction of the differences.

Therefore, the framework is best used to explain the existence and contingency of employment differentiation, rather than the subsidiary advantage itself.

90.2. Subsidiary autonomy and differentiated multinational networks

Multinational subsidiaries are not simply passive recipients of headquarters directives.

They may possess substantial autonomy, resources, capabilities, and strategic responsibilities.

Established references

  • Ghoshal, S., & Bartlett, C. A. (1990). “The Multinational Corporation as an Interorganizational Network.” Academy of Management Review, 15(4), 603–626.
  • Birkinshaw, J., Hood, N., & Jonsson, S. (1998). “Building Firm-Specific Advantages in Multinational Corporations: The Role of Subsidiary Initiative.” Strategic Management Journal, 19(3), 221–241.
  • Birkinshaw, J., & Hood, N. (1998). “Multinational Subsidiary Evolution: Capability and Charter Change in Foreign-Owned Subsidiary Companies.” Academy of Management Review, 23(4), 773–795.

These contributions emphasize that the multinational enterprise is internally differentiated.

Subsidiaries may differ in:

  • Strategic roles.
  • Capabilities.
  • Decision authority.
  • External network relationships.
  • Access to organizational resources.
  • Degree of dependence on headquarters.

This perspective is particularly relevant because the study finds substantial company-level and geographic heterogeneity.

Application to managerial experience

Subsidiary autonomy could influence employee experience in competing ways.

Potentially favorable effects

Autonomy may allow local managers to:

  • Respond quickly to employee concerns.
  • Adapt work arrangements.
  • Simplify decision-making.
  • Reduce unnecessary corporate procedures.

Potentially unfavorable effects

Autonomy may also:

  • Reduce access to headquarters resources.
  • Weaken internal career opportunities.
  • Increase local managerial discretion.
  • Generate inconsistencies in employment practices.

Therefore, the predicted relationship between autonomy and employee experience is theoretically ambiguous.

The data offer an opportunity to resolve part of this ambiguity empirically.

A potentially important mechanism

One possibility is that subsidiary autonomy moderates the relationship between foreign location and employee management evaluations.

Under this interpretation, the strongest subsidiary advantages should appear where local units possess sufficient authority to adapt management practices while retaining access to multinational resources.

However, subsidiary autonomy is not measured in the workbook.

It would need to be obtained from additional sources or constructed using validated organizational indicators.

91. Organizational centrality: the hidden costs of headquarters location

The headquarters-subsidiary findings raise a question that is not adequately resolved by conventional assumptions about central organizational positions.

Headquarters employees may enjoy advantages associated with proximity to:

  • Senior executives.
  • Corporate strategy.
  • Internal networks.
  • Organizational resources.
  • Promotion opportunities.
  • Executive decision-making.

Yet the results show that these employees also report substantially more negative organizational and managerial experiences.

This suggests that organizational centrality may confer both advantages and disadvantages.

91.1. Differentiated organizational roles

An important international business reference is:

  • Hedlund, G. (1986). “The Hypermodern MNC: A Heterarchy?” Human Resource Management, 25(1), 9–35.

Hedlund challenges the simple hierarchical conception of multinational organization.

The heterarchy perspective emphasizes distributed authority, differentiated organizational roles, and complex relationships among units.

This is relevant because headquarters and subsidiaries may face different combinations of:

  • Decision rights.
  • Information flows.
  • Accountability.
  • Coordination responsibilities.
  • Organizational uncertainty.

A headquarters position is not necessarily an uncomplicated employment advantage.

It may also involve greater exposure to organizational complexity.

91.2. Information-processing theory

Two particularly relevant organizational-theory contributions are:

  • Galbraith, J. R. (1974). “Organization Design: An Information Processing View.” Interfaces, 4(3), 28–36.
  • Galbraith, J. R. (1977). Organization Design. Addison-Wesley.

Information-processing theory proposes that organizations must develop structures capable of handling uncertainty and information-processing requirements.

Applied to multinational enterprises, this suggests that employees involved in global coordination may encounter greater informational and administrative complexity.

Headquarters employees may be particularly exposed to:

  • Cross-unit dependencies.
  • Strategic uncertainty.
  • Resource-allocation conflicts.
  • Organizational restructuring.
  • Multiple reporting relationships.

Connection with the data

The evidence is consistent with this mechanism.

The main headquarters disadvantages concern:

  • Organizational change.
  • Business strategy.
  • Organizational hierarchy.
  • Job security.
  • Bureaucracy.
  • Leadership communication.
  • Organizational scale.

These are not random workplace themes.

They are closely related to organizational complexity and decision processes.

A potentially distinctive contribution

The study could introduce the idea that organizational centrality has an employee-experience cost.

A central corporate position may offer greater access to strategic resources while imposing additional exposure to organizational complexity.

This would create a potentially important tension between:

  • The organizational advantages of centrality.
  • The employee-experience consequences of centrality.

However, the domestic border test complicates this interpretation.

The foreign organizational advantage is substantially stronger than the advantage observed in other US states.

Therefore, centrality alone cannot be assumed to explain the findings.

The theory must account for the distinctive role of international location.

92. Headquarters attention, subsidiary relationships, and organizational experience

The relationship between headquarters and subsidiaries is itself a well-established topic in international business research.

Two relevant references are:

  • Bouquet, C., & Birkinshaw, J. (2008). “Weight Versus Voice: How Foreign Subsidiaries Gain Attention from Corporate Headquarters.” Academy of Management Journal, 51(3), 577–601.
  • Ambos, T. C., Andersson, U., & Birkinshaw, J. (2010). “What Are the Consequences of Initiative-Taking in Multinational Subsidiaries?” Journal of International Business Studies, 41, 1099–1118.

These studies highlight how subsidiary relationships with headquarters involve attention, initiative, and organizational influence.

The broader literature suggests that headquarters-subsidiary relationships may differ substantially across units.

For employment research, the implication is that local organizational experiences could depend on:

  • The amount of headquarters attention.
  • The character of headquarters intervention.
  • Subsidiary strategic importance.
  • Local management discretion.
  • The intensity of reporting obligations.

Competing predictions

Greater headquarters involvement could improve subsidiary employment conditions by providing:

  • Resources.
  • Professional development.
  • Administrative support.
  • Career opportunities.

Alternatively, it could reduce perceived organizational quality by increasing:

  • Reporting demands.
  • Bureaucracy.
  • Strategic interference.
  • Uncertainty.

This ambiguity is useful.

It suggests that the relevant variable may not simply be headquarters involvement, but the form of headquarters involvement.

For example, resource-providing involvement may differ from monitoring-oriented involvement.

Proposed conceptual distinction

The study could distinguish:

  1. Supportive headquarters involvement, which provides resources and capabilities.
  2. Controlling headquarters involvement, which imposes monitoring and compliance requirements.

These mechanisms could predict different employee experiences.

However, the workbook does not directly measure headquarters involvement.

This should therefore be treated as a proposed extension rather than a conclusion drawn from the existing data.

93. International HRM: global standardization versus local adaptation

The study's employment-related outcomes naturally connect with international human resource management.

A central issue in international HRM concerns the transfer and adaptation of HR practices across national environments.

93.1. HR practice transfer

Relevant established references include:

  • Rosenzweig, P. M., & Nohria, N. (1994). “Influences on Human Resource Management Practices in Multinational Corporations.” Journal of International Business Studies, 25(2), 229–251.
  • Taylor, S., Beechler, S., & Napier, N. (1996). “Toward an Integrative Model of Strategic International Human Resource Management.” Academy of Management Review, 21(4), 959–985.
  • Kostova, T. (1999). “Transnational Transfer of Strategic Organizational Practices: A Contextual Perspective.” Academy of Management Review, 24(2), 308–324.

These contributions establish that multinational HR practices are influenced by multiple institutional and organizational pressures.

The relevant mechanisms include:

  • Headquarters-origin practices.
  • Host-country requirements.
  • Local legitimacy.
  • Internal organizational consistency.
  • Subsidiary characteristics.
  • Practice transfer and implementation.

Application to the findings

The evidence suggests that formal corporate membership does not guarantee equivalent employee experiences.

For example, the same multinational may exhibit:

  • Similar compensation narratives but different management narratives.
  • Similar technology experiences but different workplace cultures.
  • Different benefits narratives despite comparable job titles.

This is consistent with the international HRM distinction between the transfer of formal practices and their local implementation.

Potential theoretical contribution

A central proposition could be:

Multinational HR standardization does not necessarily imply employee-experience standardization.

This distinction is important.

A firm may use common HR systems across countries while employees experience those systems differently.

Such differences may arise from local implementation, employee expectations, or the surrounding institutional environment.

However, the present data do not directly measure HR standardization.

The contribution would therefore require an empirical extension linking employee narratives to firm-level or subsidiary-level HR practices.

94. Institutional duality: subsidiaries operate between two environments

One of the most relevant theoretical concepts is institutional duality.

Foreign subsidiaries are embedded simultaneously in:

  1. The organizational environment of the multinational enterprise.
  2. The institutional environment of the host country.

A foundational reference is:

  • Kostova, T., & Roth, K. (2002). “Adoption of an Organizational Practice by Subsidiaries of Multinational Corporations: Institutional and Relational Effects.” Academy of Management Journal, 45(1), 215–233.

This perspective helps explain why subsidiaries may share corporate ownership while experiencing different employment arrangements.

The same organizational practice may encounter different:

  • Institutional expectations.
  • Regulatory requirements.
  • Normative environments.
  • Labor-market conditions.
  • Social expectations.

94.1. Why this matters for the border test

The domestic border test is especially important here.

If simple geographic separation from headquarters were sufficient to generate the observed differences, other domestic establishments might exhibit similar patterns.

However, the foreign comparison shows much stronger managerial and organizational advantages.

This is consistent with the possibility that crossing national institutional boundaries matters.

The result does not directly identify institutional duality as the cause.

Nevertheless, it makes institutional explanations more plausible than a purely domestic organizational-distance interpretation.

94.2. Why this matters for benefits

The benefits findings are particularly compatible with institutional duality.

Employer-provided benefits are embedded in national institutions.

For example, countries differ in the respective roles of:

  • Public social insurance.
  • Employer-sponsored health coverage.
  • Occupational retirement programs.
  • Statutory leave.
  • Collective bargaining.
  • Employment protection.

Consequently, the same multinational may offer different benefit packages across countries without necessarily violating internal organizational consistency.

Employee expectations may also differ.

This means that benefits mentions may reflect both actual employment arrangements and national reference standards.

94.3. Why this matters for management

Institutional duality may also affect managerial behavior.

Local management practices may reflect:

  • National communication norms.
  • Leadership expectations.
  • Employee voice institutions.
  • Employment protection.
  • Organizational legitimacy requirements.

However, such effects need not be universally positive.

The regional reversals in processes and working time indicate that institutional adaptation can be associated with both favorable and unfavorable outcomes.

95. Comparative capitalism and national employment systems

The contrast between benefits and management also connects with comparative institutional research.

A foundational reference is:

  • Hall, P. A., & Soskice, D. (2001). Varieties of Capitalism: The Institutional Foundations of Comparative Advantage. Oxford University Press.

The varieties-of-capitalism framework emphasizes the role of national institutions in shaping firm behavior and employment relationships.

Although the framework was not developed specifically to explain Glassdoor review differences, it provides a useful foundation for examining international employment systems.

Potential application

The observed benefits differences may depend on the national organization of:

  • Social protection.
  • Employment regulation.
  • Labor-market coordination.
  • Skill development.
  • Collective bargaining.

The same corporate employer may therefore operate within different employment systems abroad.

Important limitation

The countries in the workbook should not be assigned mechanically to simple institutional categories.

Moreover, the empirical results do not establish that national institutions explain the observed differences.

A rigorous test would require quantitative institutional indicators.

Examples include:

  • Public social expenditure.
  • Employment protection measures.
  • Collective bargaining coverage.
  • Employer social contributions.
  • Statutory leave entitlements.
  • Labor-market coordination indicators.

These would allow the study to move beyond country labels toward measurable institutional mechanisms.

96. Labor economics: multinational wage premiums and segmented labor markets

The compensation findings require a different theoretical perspective.

International business research has long examined employment differences between domestic firms and multinational employers.

However, the present study asks a more specific question:

How do employment experiences differ between headquarters and foreign subsidiaries of the same multinational?

This is not equivalent to comparing multinational employers with domestic employers.

Nevertheless, the foreign-ownership wage literature provides relevant mechanisms.

96.1. Foreign ownership and wages

Established references include:

  • Aitken, B., Harrison, A., & Lipsey, R. E. (1996). “Wages and Foreign Ownership: A Comparative Study of Mexico, Venezuela, and the United States.” Journal of International Economics, 40(3–4), 345–371.
  • Lipsey, R. E., & Sjöholm, F. (2004). “Foreign Direct Investment, Education and Wages in Indonesian Manufacturing.” Journal of Development Economics, 73(1), 415–422.
  • Heyman, F., Sjöholm, F., & Tingvall, P. G. (2007). “Is There Really a Foreign Ownership Wage Premium? Evidence from Matched Employer-Employee Data.” Journal of International Economics, 73(2), 355–376.

This literature examines whether foreign-owned employers offer different wages and how observed differences may reflect worker and firm selection.

Application to the current results

The foreign-ownership wage-premium perspective may help explain why employees in some subsidiaries rate employment favorably relative to local alternatives.

However, it does not necessarily predict that foreign subsidiaries pay more than their multinational headquarters.

This distinction is essential.

The comparison in the present study is internal to the multinational.

Employees in subsidiary countries may evaluate pay relative to local labor markets, whereas headquarters employees evaluate pay relative to different domestic alternatives.

Consequently, subjective compensation evaluations may differ even when absolute or purchasing-power-adjusted wages do not.

Implication

The compensation results should ideally be supplemented with:

  • Local market wage benchmarks.
  • Occupational average wages.
  • Purchasing-power-adjusted compensation.
  • Company-specific pay levels.
  • Employee benefits valuation.

This would allow the study to distinguish absolute rewards from relative labor-market positioning.

97. Efficiency wages and the multinational employment relationship

Another relevant labor-economics framework concerns efficiency wages.

A foundational reference is:

  • Akerlof, G. A., & Yellen, J. L. (Eds.). (1986). Efficiency Wage Models of the Labor Market. Cambridge University Press.

Efficiency-wage approaches examine why firms may offer compensation above the market-clearing level.

Potential mechanisms include:

  • Worker effort.
  • Employee retention.
  • Monitoring costs.
  • Recruitment.
  • Productivity.

Application

Multinational subsidiaries may seek to recruit and retain skilled employees in host-country labor markets.

This can create incentives to offer employment packages that are attractive relative to local alternatives.

However, the incentives may differ by occupation.

For example:

  • Specialized technical employees may be relatively scarce.
  • Financial-sector employees may possess internationally transferable skills.
  • Operational workers may face different labor-market conditions.

This is consistent with the highly heterogeneous compensation results.

However, the present study does not measure actual wages or productivity.

Efficiency-wage theory is therefore a potential explanation rather than an established mechanism.

98. Labor-market segmentation and occupational heterogeneity

The occupational results suggest that professional and operational employees may experience different headquarters-subsidiary configurations.

This relates to labor-market segmentation.

A foundational reference is:

  • Doeringer, P. B., & Piore, M. J. (1971). Internal Labor Markets and Manpower Analysis. D. C. Heath.

The internal labor-market perspective emphasizes that employment arrangements are structured by organizational rules, promotion systems, and occupational positions.

Application

Within a multinational, employees may occupy different internal labor markets.

For example:

  • Corporate professional employees may have access to internal mobility networks.
  • Subsidiary professionals may gain local learning opportunities.
  • Operational employees may face different scheduling and workload systems.
  • Managers may receive different rewards and responsibilities depending on organizational location.

This perspective is compatible with the observed distinction between:

  • Learning through work.
  • Internal career mobility.
  • Compensation.
  • Benefits.
  • Work intensity.

Potential contribution

The study could argue that international organizational location interacts with occupational segmentation to produce distinct employment experiences.

However, this claim requires formal evidence that the occupational differences persist within comparable companies and host countries.

99. Social comparison theory: employees evaluate their jobs relative to alternatives

The study's review-based measures introduce a fundamental social-psychological issue.

Employees do not evaluate employment conditions in an absolute vacuum.

Their judgments depend partly on reference groups and expectations.

A foundational reference is:

  • Festinger, L. (1954). “A Theory of Social Comparison Processes.” Human Relations, 7(2), 117–140.

Social comparison theory proposes that individuals evaluate themselves and their circumstances partly through comparisons with others.

For the present study, this suggests that headquarters and subsidiary employees may use different reference groups.

99.1. Headquarters reference groups

Headquarters employees may compare their experiences with:

  • Other large domestic corporations.
  • Competing employers.
  • Local professional opportunities.
  • Domestic compensation standards.

99.2. Subsidiary reference groups

Foreign subsidiary employees may compare their experiences with:

  • Domestic firms in the host country.
  • Other multinational subsidiaries.
  • Local employment norms.
  • Available career alternatives.

These comparisons may affect how employees evaluate identical nominal employment characteristics.

99.3. Connection with the data

This mechanism is particularly relevant to:

  • Overall ratings.
  • Culture evaluations.
  • Compensation satisfaction.
  • Benefits praise.
  • Employer prestige.
  • Learning opportunities.

For example, the same compensation package might be evaluated differently depending on the local market benchmark.

Similarly, multinational employment may carry different professional significance across countries.

Critical limitation

Reference-group theory alone does not explain why management and organizational complaints are consistently lower abroad.

It could contribute, but the direction of the effect depends on employees' actual comparison standards.

Therefore, reference groups should be treated as one component of a broader explanation.

100. Organizational identification and perceived employer status

Foreign subsidiary employees may experience multinational membership differently from headquarters employees.

Two established organizational-theory references are especially relevant:

  • Ashforth, B. E., & Mael, F. (1989). “Social Identity Theory and the Organization.” Academy of Management Review, 14(1), 20–39.
  • Dutton, J. E., Dukerich, J. M., & Harquail, C. V. (1994). “Organizational Images and Member Identification.” Administrative Science Quarterly, 39(2), 239–263.

These contributions explain how organizational identity and perceived organizational image can influence employees' identification with their employer.

Application to multinational subsidiary employment

A multinational may possess different status in different labor markets.

In some host countries, employment in a large multinational may be associated with:

  • Professional prestige.
  • International career opportunities.
  • Advanced organizational practices.
  • Training opportunities.
  • Employer reputation.

At headquarters, the same employer competes with other large domestic organizations.

This could generate different employee evaluations.

Connection with the results

The strongest relevant findings include:

  • Higher overall ratings abroad.
  • Greater positive workplace-culture references.
  • More favorable learning-through-work references.
  • Greater management and organizational net advantages.

However, the observed management and organizational differences are frequently complaint-driven.

Organizational identification alone may not adequately explain why negative organizational themes are less frequent.

Moreover, the company-spread results indicate that the pattern is widespread but not universal.

Proposed mechanism

An employee's perception of the multinational's standing in the local labor market may moderate the relationship between subsidiary employment and workplace evaluations.

This would be particularly relevant when the multinational offers distinctive employment opportunities relative to domestic alternatives.

The mechanism could be tested using measures of local employer prestige, labor-market wages, or perceived organizational status.

However, no such measures are currently included in the workbook.

101. Psychological contract theory: different expectations within the same multinational

The psychological contract perspective is particularly relevant to the benefits and management findings.

A foundational reference is:

  • Rousseau, D. M. (1989). “Psychological and Implied Contracts in Organizations.” Employee Responsibilities and Rights Journal, 2(2), 121–139.

Psychological contract theory examines employees' beliefs concerning the reciprocal obligations between themselves and their employer.

Application

Headquarters and subsidiary employees may enter employment with different expectations concerning:

  • Compensation.
  • Benefits.
  • Career development.
  • Managerial support.
  • Job security.
  • Work-life balance.

Even where formal employment policies are similar, differences in expectations may affect perceived fulfillment.

For example, headquarters employees may place greater emphasis on benefits and internal career mobility.

Subsidiary employees may place greater emphasis on managerial treatment, developmental opportunities, or employer reputation.

Connection with the findings

The data exhibit different patterns of praise and complaint across domains.

This is compatible with differences in psychological contracts.

However, the text rates do not directly measure expectations or contract fulfillment.

Therefore, the psychological contract mechanism remains hypothetical.

A stronger study could use review-level language concerning:

  • Promises.
  • Expectations.
  • Fairness.
  • Betrayal.
  • Trust.
  • Reciprocity.

The codebook may already contain relevant themes, but the current aggregate outcomes do not directly measure psychological-contract breach.

102. Organizational justice: explaining management and employee-treatment differences

Organizational justice theory provides another relevant perspective.

A foundational review is:

  • Colquitt, J. A., Conlon, D. E., Wesson, M. J., Porter, C. O. L. H., & Ng, K. Y. (2001). “Justice at the Millennium: A Meta-Analytic Review of 25 Years of Organizational Justice Research.” Journal of Applied Psychology, 86(3), 425–445.

Organizational justice distinguishes different dimensions of perceived fairness.

These include:

  • Distributive justice.
  • Procedural justice.
  • Interpersonal justice.
  • Informational justice.

This distinction is especially useful for the present data.

102.1. Distributive justice

Relevant to:

  • Compensation.
  • Benefits.
  • Pay equity.
  • Rewards.

102.2. Procedural justice

Relevant to:

  • Performance appraisal.
  • Promotion processes.
  • Bureaucracy.
  • Decision-making.
  • Employee voice.

102.3. Interpersonal justice

Relevant to:

  • Employee treatment.
  • Management support.
  • Workplace relationships.
  • Favoritism.

102.4. Informational justice

Relevant to:

  • Leadership communication.
  • Organizational transparency.
  • Management accessibility.

Why this matters

The aggregate management advantage contains several distinct justice-related themes.

For example:

  • Employee treatment and welfare.
  • Leadership communication.
  • Favoritism.
  • Performance appraisal.
  • Employee voice.

The results suggest fewer negative references to these experiences in foreign subsidiaries.

However, the processes and coordination domain sometimes favors headquarters.

This raises the possibility that interpersonal and procedural experiences differ.

For example, some subsidiaries may exhibit favorable employee treatment while having less favorable bureaucratic procedures.

The Middle Eastern regional results provide a preliminary illustration.

Theoretical opportunity

One potentially important contribution would be to show that foreign subsidiary employment is associated with different dimensions of perceived organizational justice rather than a uniform management-quality difference.

However, a justice interpretation would require careful mapping of codebook themes to established justice constructs.

The mapping should be validated rather than assumed.

103. Job demands-resources theory: why culture and workload may diverge

The occupational results identify a particularly interesting pattern.

Some subsidiary employees report:

  • Favorable culture.
  • Favorable management.
  • Unfavorable workload.
  • Unfavorable working time.

This is consistent with the distinction between job demands and job resources.

A foundational reference is:

  • Demerouti, E., Bakker, A. B., Nachreiner, F., & Schaufeli, W. B. (2001). “The Job Demands-Resources Model of Burnout.” Journal of Applied Psychology, 86(3), 499–512.

The job demands-resources framework distinguishes between:

Job demands

  • Workload.
  • Time pressure.
  • Emotional demands.
  • Physical demands.
  • Role complexity.

Job resources

  • Social support.
  • Autonomy.
  • Feedback.
  • Development opportunities.
  • Supervisory support.

Application

A foreign subsidiary could provide stronger relational resources while simultaneously imposing greater work demands.

This would produce exactly the type of multidimensional configuration observed among some operational employees.

For example:

  • Singapore exhibits favorable management and culture but unfavorable workload.
  • Warehouse and manufacturing employees exhibit favorable culture but unfavorable workload.
  • Frontline workers show favorable culture alongside a negative workload net difference.

Theoretical significance

This framework provides a disciplined explanation for why positive employee evaluations can coexist with demanding work.

It is also more appropriate than interpreting these results as contradictory.

A workplace can provide substantial social and organizational resources while still imposing high job demands.

Limitation

The data do not establish that job resources compensate for job demands.

Nor do they directly measure burnout, engagement, or performance.

Therefore, the current results support a descriptive distinction between resources and demands, not a causal buffering mechanism.

104. Person-organization fit and institutional expectations

Another relevant framework concerns person-organization fit.

A foundational reference is:

  • Kristof, A. L. (1996). “Person-Organization Fit: An Integrative Review of Its Conceptualizations, Measurement, and Implications.” Personnel Psychology, 49(1), 1–49.

Person-organization fit concerns compatibility between employees and organizational environments.

The theory suggests that employee evaluations may depend partly on the alignment between:

  • Employee values.
  • Organizational practices.
  • Workplace culture.
  • Expectations.
  • Work arrangements.

A multinational's corporate culture may fit employee expectations differently across countries.

This could partly explain why the same organization receives different cultural evaluations at headquarters and abroad.

However, the present study does not directly measure employee values or organizational fit.

The framework is therefore more useful as a possible micro-level mediator than as the central theory.

105. Integrating the theories: four distinct mechanisms

The theoretical literature suggests four candidate mechanisms.

These mechanisms should be distinguished rather than combined indiscriminately.

Mechanism 1. Organizational centrality and exposure

Core argument

Headquarters employees are more directly exposed to corporate strategy, restructuring, and coordination complexity.

Relevant theories

  • Information-processing theory.
  • Multinational heterarchy.
  • Organizational network perspectives.

Most relevant findings

  • Organizational change.
  • Job security.
  • Bureaucracy.
  • Senior leadership.
  • Organizational structure.
  • Strategy.

Key prediction

The headquarters-subsidiary organizational gap should be larger in jobs with greater corporate coordination responsibilities.

Main alternative explanation

Headquarters employees may simply possess more information about organizational problems.

Mechanism 2. Institutional and managerial localization

Core argument

Foreign subsidiaries adapt workplace and managerial practices to host-country environments.

Relevant theories

  • Integration-responsiveness.
  • Institutional duality.
  • International HRM practice transfer.

Most relevant findings

  • Geographic heterogeneity.
  • Management differences.
  • Culture differences.
  • Benefits variation.
  • Domestic-versus-international border contrast.

Key prediction

The headquarters-subsidiary difference should depend on host-country institutions and subsidiary autonomy.

Main alternative explanation

Different employee expectations may generate similar reporting patterns without equivalent differences in actual practices.

Mechanism 3. Local labor-market reference groups

Core argument

Employees evaluate multinational employment relative to locally available alternatives.

Relevant theories

  • Social comparison.
  • Organizational identification.
  • Foreign-ownership labor-market research.
  • Psychological contracts.

Most relevant findings

  • Higher overall ratings abroad.
  • Strong culture differences.
  • Mixed compensation differences.
  • Benefits salience.
  • Learning and career differences.

Key prediction

The subsidiary rating advantage should be larger where multinational employment offers a stronger relative advantage over domestic labor-market alternatives.

Main alternative explanation

Actual working conditions may differ independently of employee reference groups.

Mechanism 4. Occupationally segmented employment demands and resources

Core argument

The consequences of multinational location differ across occupational groups because jobs involve different combinations of demands and resources.

Relevant theories

  • Internal labor markets.
  • Job demands-resources.
  • Organizational justice.
  • International HRM.

Most relevant findings

  • Professional versus frontline differences.
  • Operational workload disadvantage.
  • Management-function advantages.
  • Sector-specific compensation reversals.

Key prediction

The location effect should interact with job function and occupational demands.

Main alternative explanation

The observed differences may arise from imperfect title matching or different firms and countries represented within functions.

106. Which theory should anchor the paper?

I would not recommend building the paper around five equally important theories.

That would risk producing an overly broad and weakly integrated contribution.

Instead, I recommend the following hierarchy.

Primary theoretical foundation

Multinational organizational differentiation and institutional duality.

This directly addresses the international business research question.

The multinational integrates activities across countries while operating within heterogeneous institutional environments.

Consequently, employee experiences may differ systematically across headquarters and subsidiaries.

Primary organizational mechanism

Organizational centrality and differential exposure to corporate complexity.

This provides a plausible explanation for the unusually strong differences in management and organizational complaints.

Primary micro-level mechanism

Local reference groups and employee expectations.

This helps explain why reported evaluations may differ across national labor markets, particularly for benefits, compensation, culture, and overall ratings.

Boundary condition

Occupational position and job demands.

This explains why professional and operational employees may experience different patterns.

Secondary mechanism

Employee tenure and cumulative exposure.

This is potentially important but currently too vulnerable to survivor selection to serve as the primary mechanism.

107. Proposed theoretical contribution 1: multinational employment differentiation

107.1. The conventional assumption being challenged

International business research has extensively examined how multinationals distribute:

  • Production.
  • Knowledge.
  • Capabilities.
  • Authority.
  • Strategic responsibilities.
  • HR practices.

However, the current findings suggest that another outcome deserves explicit theoretical attention:

The distribution of employee experiences across organizational locations.

Common corporate ownership does not imply equivalent employment experiences.

Nor does a common job title guarantee equivalent exposure to corporate practices.

The study could therefore extend research on differentiated multinational networks to the employee-experience level.

107.2. Proposed conceptualization

Define multinational employment differentiation as:

Systematic variation in employees' experienced or reported employment conditions across organizational locations belonging to the same multinational enterprise, holding constant important aspects of employer identity and nominal occupational role.

This concept would encompass:

  • Material rewards.
  • Managerial relationships.
  • Organizational processes.
  • Cultural experiences.
  • Career opportunities.
  • Work demands.

The contribution is not simply that working conditions differ.

That would be unsurprising given national institutional differences.

The stronger contribution is that the differences have a structured, multidimensional pattern and are not reducible to a single favorable or unfavorable location effect.

107.3. Why the current evidence is useful

The data show:

  • A strong management advantage abroad.
  • A strong organizational advantage abroad.
  • A substantial benefits-praise advantage at headquarters.
  • Mixed compensation differences.
  • Occupationally contingent workload differences.
  • Larger foreign than domestic organizational gaps.

This is consistent with the idea that the multinational distributes different employment advantages across organizational locations.

107.4. What would make the contribution credible?

The contribution would become substantially stronger if:

  1. The main findings survive no-cons corrections.
  2. They survive classification-error adjustments.
  3. They remain after excluding India.
  4. They survive equal-company weighting.
  5. They persist on a common text-and-rating matched sample.
  6. They remain after controlling for job function and national institutions.

Without these tests, the concept risks describing differences in review-writing rather than employment conditions.

108. Proposed theoretical contribution 2: the employee-experience consequences of organizational centrality

A second potential contribution concerns the costs and benefits of headquarters location.

The conventional view emphasizes the strategic importance of headquarters.

However, strategic centrality may also expose employees to organizational complexity.

Proposed argument

Headquarters employees may enjoy greater proximity to the firm's strategic resources while encountering greater exposure to:

  • Organizational restructuring.
  • Corporate politics.
  • Strategic uncertainty.
  • Coordination burdens.
  • Senior leadership changes.

This could explain why headquarters employees report more negative organizational experiences.

Evidence supporting the argument

The overall theme-level results show higher headquarters complaints concerning:

  • Organizational change.
  • Job security.
  • Business strategy.
  • Organizational scale.
  • Management quality.
  • Leadership communication.

These themes are consistent with organizational complexity.

Evidence limiting the argument

The domestic-versus-international border test shows that foreign establishments exhibit much larger organizational advantages than other domestic establishments.

Thus, organizational centrality is unlikely to be a complete explanation.

The contribution would be stronger if framed as:

The employee-experience consequences of headquarters centrality depend on the institutional and organizational context in which nonheadquarters units operate.

This explicitly links organizational theory and international business.

109. Proposed theoretical contribution 3: separating material and relational employment quality

The third potential contribution involves the multidimensional character of employment quality.

Rather than treating employment quality as one continuum, the study distinguishes:

  1. Material employment rewards.
  2. Relational and cultural experiences.
  3. Organizational processes.
  4. Career development.
  5. Work demands.

Empirical motivation

The clearest contrast is between:

  • Management: +6.75 pp.
  • Organization: +6.98 pp.
  • Culture: +5.43 pp.
  • Benefits: −6.62 pp.

The first three favor subsidiaries.

The fourth favors headquarters.

Theoretical interpretation

Different employment dimensions may respond to different mechanisms.

Material rewards may depend more strongly on:

  • National institutions.
  • Labor-market conditions.
  • Compensation structures.
  • Tenure.
  • Employment contracts.

Relational and organizational experiences may depend more strongly on:

  • Local management.
  • Organizational autonomy.
  • Integration demands.
  • Corporate coordination.
  • Workplace norms.

The study could contribute by demonstrating that employment conditions within multinationals are differentiated across dimensions rather than consistently ranked across locations.

Important caution

The data currently establish a difference in reported thematic frequencies.

They do not establish a compensating employment bargain.

The word tradeoff should therefore be used cautiously.

A defensible expression is:

Contrasting relational and material employment asymmetries.

110. Proposed theoretical contribution 4: occupationally contingent multinational employment

The fourth contribution concerns occupational heterogeneity.

The results suggest that professional and managerial employees experience strong organizational advantages abroad.

Operational and frontline employees sometimes experience less favorable workload or working-time arrangements.

This could extend international HRM research by showing that foreign subsidiary employment is shaped jointly by:

  • Organizational location.
  • National institutional environment.
  • Occupational position.

The implication is that the same multinational may generate different location-related employment experiences for different types of workers.

However, the current evidence is not sufficient to claim a general occupational hierarchy.

The smaller operational samples and title-comparability concerns require further analysis.

I would therefore treat this as a boundary condition rather than a separate central contribution.

111. Proposed theoretical contribution 5: tenure-contingent employment differentiation

The tenure results suggest that the headquarters-subsidiary difference may become more pronounced with organizational membership duration.

However, this is particularly vulnerable to selection.

Potential contribution

If replicated, the findings could suggest that the effects of organizational location accumulate over time.

This would connect multinational organization research with employee socialization, organizational tenure, and retention.

The possible mechanisms include:

  • Accumulated organizational exposure.
  • Changing expectations.
  • Greater managerial responsibilities.
  • Organizational identification.
  • Selective retention.

Why caution is necessary

The dataset does not track the same employees longitudinally.

Therefore, the observed tenure gradient cannot establish within-person development.

A theory based on cumulative experience would require evidence distinguishing exposure accumulation from employee selection.

Until then, tenure should be treated as a promising moderator rather than a demonstrated dynamic mechanism.

112. Proposed integrated model

The most promising integrated explanation is as follows.

Multinational organizational location is associated with employees' reported employment experiences through three broad processes.

Process A. Organizational exposure

Headquarters and subsidiaries differ in their exposure to corporate complexity, decision-making, and organizational change.

This should be particularly relevant to managerial and organizational complaints.

Process B. Institutional embedding

Foreign subsidiaries operate within host-country employment institutions and adapt corporate practices accordingly.

This should be particularly relevant to benefits, management practices, and working-time arrangements.

Process C. Reference-group evaluation

Employees evaluate their conditions relative to locally available alternatives and expectations.

This should be particularly relevant to compensation satisfaction, workplace culture, employer prestige, and overall ratings.

The importance of these processes depends on:

  • Host-country context.
  • Occupational function.
  • Organizational hierarchy.
  • Employment status.
  • Employee tenure.
  • Subsidiary autonomy.

The expected result is not a uniform subsidiary advantage.

Instead, the firm generates different configurations of employment experiences across locations.

113. Proposed hypotheses

I recommend developing six hypotheses, but not treating all six as equally central.

The first three could form the core empirical argument.

The remaining three would address mechanisms and boundary conditions.

H1. Foreign subsidiary managerial and organizational experience

Hypothesis 1

Within the same multinational enterprise and normalized job title, employees in foreign subsidiaries report a more favorable balance of managerial and organizational experiences than employees at headquarters.

Rationale

Foreign subsidiaries may differ from headquarters in their exposure to corporate complexity and in their implementation of local managerial practices.

Empirical support

Overall net differences:

  • Management: +6.75 pp.
  • Organization: +6.98 pp.

Higher subsidiary ratings:

  • Senior Management: +0.208.
  • Culture & Values: +0.256.

Important refinement

The hypothesis should distinguish:

  • Higher praise.
  • Lower complaints.

The existing evidence suggests that the main effect is primarily complaint-driven.

A more precise version could therefore focus on negative organizational experiences.

H2. International-border specificity

Hypothesis 2

The favorable managerial and organizational experience gap associated with nonheadquarters employment is larger for foreign subsidiaries than for domestic establishments outside the headquarters location.

Rationale

International institutional embedding and subsidiary-specific organizational arrangements may generate differences beyond generic geographic separation from headquarters.

Empirical support

Management net gaps:

  • Foreign: +6.82 pp.
  • Other US state: +1.18 pp.

Organization net gaps:

  • Foreign: +7.00 pp.
  • Other US state: −0.65 pp.

Required test

A direct foreign-versus-domestic interaction with comparable company-job composition and clustered uncertainty.

This hypothesis is particularly important because it provides the paper's clearest link to international business theory.

H3. Differentiation between relational and material employment conditions

Hypothesis 3

Relative to headquarters employees, foreign subsidiary employees report more favorable relational and organizational experiences but less favorable positive evaluations of nonwage benefits.

Rationale

Relational experiences and benefits may be shaped by different organizational and institutional processes.

Empirical support

  • Management: +6.75 pp.
  • Organization: +6.98 pp.
  • Culture: +5.43 pp.
  • Benefits: −6.62 pp.

Important qualification

The benefits result concerns positive thematic mentions, not verified differences in objective benefit generosity.

The hypothesis should therefore remain explicitly focused on reported experiences unless objective evidence is added.

H4. Host-country institutional moderation

Hypothesis 4

The magnitude and composition of headquarters-subsidiary employment-experience differences vary systematically with host-country institutional conditions.

Rationale

Foreign subsidiaries operate under heterogeneous employment institutions and managerial expectations.

Empirical support

The observed regional and country differences are substantial.

For example:

  • Asia exhibits strong management and organization advantages.
  • Europe exhibits stronger culture differences and less favorable coordination.
  • North America exhibits smaller organizational differences.

Required test

Replace broad country labels with measurable institutional indicators.

Potential moderators include:

  • Labor-market institutions.
  • Social protection.
  • Employment protection.
  • Collective bargaining.
  • Local wage structures.
  • Institutional distance from headquarters.

The direction of individual institutional effects should be specified after developing a more precise theoretical argument.

H5. Occupational moderation

Hypothesis 5

The headquarters-subsidiary difference in reported employment experiences varies across occupational functions, with professional and managerial roles exhibiting stronger organizational advantages and operational roles exhibiting less favorable work-demand differences.

Rationale

Different occupational groups face different configurations of organizational responsibilities, resources, and job demands.

Empirical support

Professional and managerial functions show strong organizational advantages.

Operational functions exhibit negative workload and working-time differences in several subgroups.

Required test

Estimate location-by-function interactions within comparable companies, countries, and periods.

The hypothesis should be tested using actual task characteristics where possible, rather than relying exclusively on broad title classifications.

H6. Tenure moderation

Hypothesis 6

The headquarters-subsidiary difference in reported managerial and organizational experiences is more favorable to subsidiaries among employees with longer organizational tenure.

Rationale

Longer tenure may increase exposure to organizational processes and differences in organizational membership experiences.

Empirical support

Management net differences:

  • 1–3 years: +4.37 pp.
  • 8+ years: +12.12 pp.

Overall rating differences:

  • Less than one year: +0.094.
  • 8+ years: +0.362.

Required test

A formal tenure interaction, ideally supplemented with methods addressing selection into continued employment.

Important limitation

The hypothesis concerns differences across tenure groups.

It does not imply that employees become more satisfied over time.

114. Hypotheses versus propositions

For an observational study of this kind, it may be useful to distinguish hypotheses from broader theoretical propositions.

The hypotheses above predict empirical associations.

However, several proposed mechanisms are not directly measured.

For those mechanisms, propositions may be more appropriate.

For example:

Proposition 1: Organizational exposure

Greater exposure to corporate-level decision-making and organizational complexity increases employees' propensity to report negative organizational experiences.

Proposition 2: Institutional localization

Host-country institutional embedding modifies the relationship between multinational organizational location and the composition of employee employment experiences.

Proposition 3: Employment multidimensionality

The international distribution of relational employment quality is not necessarily aligned with the distribution of material employment rewards.

Proposition 4: Occupational segmentation

Organizational location interacts with occupational task characteristics to produce distinct configurations of managerial resources and job demands.

These propositions are broader than the directly observed contrasts.

They would require new measures and additional analyses to test.

115. Recommended theoretical architecture for the paper

I recommend avoiding an overly complicated conceptual framework.

A relatively parsimonious structure would be stronger.

Core phenomenon

Multidimensional headquarters-subsidiary employment differentiation.

Primary mechanism

Differential organizational exposure and local managerial implementation.

International boundary condition

Host-country institutional embedding.

Employee-level boundary condition

Occupational role.

Dynamic extension

Tenure, conditional on addressing selection.

Principal outcomes

  • Management and organizational complaints.
  • Workplace culture.
  • Benefits praise.
  • Overall employee ratings.

This architecture would allow the paper to make a coherent international business contribution while retaining the richness of the data.

116. Which theoretical contribution is most publishable?

The strongest candidate is not simply that foreign subsidiaries receive higher Glassdoor ratings.

That descriptive relationship alone may be insufficiently novel.

Nor is the strongest contribution that countries differ in their employment conditions.

That observation is already well established.

The more distinctive contribution concerns the structured distribution of employee experiences within the same multinational and nominally the same job.

The most promising theoretical statement is:

Multinational enterprises do not merely differentiate strategic activities and organizational capabilities across countries. They also generate differentiated employee experiences, with foreign subsidiaries often exhibiting more favorable relational and organizational conditions while headquarters employees report stronger benefits-related advantages. These asymmetries depend on institutional context and occupational position.

The theoretical importance lies in connecting:

  1. Multinational organizational differentiation.
  2. International institutional embedding.
  3. Employee-level experiences.
  4. Occupational heterogeneity.

The paper would thereby extend the study of multinational organizational structure to the conditions under which employees experience multinational employment.

What would make the contribution particularly strong?

The most compelling empirical result would be demonstrating that:

The managerial and organizational subsidiary advantage remains after controlling for review selection, job comparability, national institutional conditions, and company composition, while the benefits difference remains substantively distinct.

If that result survives, the theoretical contribution would be considerably stronger.

117. Alternative theoretical interpretations that must remain open

A strong paper should not commit prematurely to one explanation.

At least four alternative interpretations remain plausible.

Interpretation A. Actual management differences

Foreign subsidiary employees genuinely experience more favorable local management practices.

Interpretation B. Differential corporate exposure

Headquarters employees encounter more organizational complexity and strategic uncertainty.

Interpretation C. Different reporting and reference standards

Employees in different countries select different themes when evaluating their employer.

Interpretation D. Employee and job selection

The matched headquarters and subsidiary reviewers differ in characteristics not captured by normalized job-title matching.

The present evidence cannot conclusively distinguish these alternatives.

Indeed, more than one may operate simultaneously.

The next stage should therefore focus on identifying analyses that can eliminate or substantially weaken competing explanations.

118. Interim theoretical conclusion

The theoretical literature suggests that the central empirical result should be understood as a problem of multinational organizational differentiation, not merely as a comparison of employee satisfaction across countries.

Foreign subsidiaries occupy distinctive organizational and institutional positions.

These positions may shape:

  • Managerial practices.
  • Exposure to corporate complexity.
  • Employee expectations.
  • Employment rewards.
  • Work demands.

The data suggest that the resulting employment experiences are multidimensional.

The strongest advantages abroad concern relational and organizational narratives.

The strongest headquarters advantage concerns benefits praise.

Other domains depend heavily on institutional and occupational context.

A theory linking organizational centrality, institutional embedding, and employee reference groups offers a promising explanation.

However, it must be tested against serious alternatives, particularly review-writing differences, employee selection, coding error, and imperfect occupational matching.

The final part of the report will therefore assess the threats to inference, specify the empirical analyses required to address them, and formulate a prioritized set of questions for international business and management experts.

PART VII. ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS, THREATS TO VALIDITY, AND THE RESEARCH AGENDA

119. What must be established before interpreting the findings as differences in working conditions?

The descriptive evidence is unusually rich. It includes matched company-job comparisons, 126 themes, 15 domains, multiple subgroup dimensions, independent star ratings, and company-level heterogeneity.

However, the central empirical distinction is between differences in reported employment experiences and differences in objectively experienced working conditions.

These are not equivalent.

The study currently demonstrates that foreign subsidiary employees describe their employment differently from headquarters employees. The strongest differences concern management, organizational functioning, workplace culture, and benefits.

The next question is whether these differences arise from:

  1. Actual differences in employment conditions.
  2. Differences in employee expectations.
  3. Differences in review-writing behavior.
  4. Differences in the employees who submit reviews.
  5. Differences in nominally matched jobs.
  6. Classification and measurement error.
  7. Differences in the firms, countries, and occupations represented in the samples.

A convincing paper must distinguish these explanations as far as the data permit.

I recommend treating this validation stage as central to the research rather than as a routine robustness appendix.

120. Threat 1: review-writing differences between headquarters and subsidiaries

120.1. Why this is probably the most important measurement threat

The principal text outcomes measure the frequency with which employees mention workplace themes.

They do not directly measure the prevalence of objectively experienced conditions.

Consider the management result:

  • HQ management complaints: 28.89%.
  • Subsidiary management complaints: 22.16%.
  • Difference: −6.73 pp.

This could indicate that subsidiary employees experience fewer management problems.

However, it could also indicate that headquarters employees are more likely to discuss management when writing a negative review.

The distinction is important because review-writing behavior may vary across countries.

Employees may differ in:

  • Willingness to criticize employers.
  • Directness of communication.
  • Expectations regarding management.
  • Length of written reviews.
  • Choice of topics.
  • Perceived consequences of criticism.
  • Familiarity with Glassdoor.
  • Motivation for submitting a review.

These differences could influence thematic frequencies even if underlying working conditions were similar.

120.2. The NO_CONS problem

The workbook identifies a particularly important reporting-style artifact.

The NO_CONS theme captures reviews in which employees explicitly report having no disadvantages to mention.

This is more common among subsidiary reviews.

Overall:

  • HQ: 3.24%.
  • Subsidiaries: 5.90%.
  • Difference: +2.66 pp.
  • 95% CI: [2.03, 3.31].

The difference is especially pronounced in India.

This matters because no-cons texts enter the denominator of the complaint-rate measure.

If a larger proportion of subsidiary complaint fields contain no substantive complaints, the measured frequency of other complaint themes may decline mechanically.

Recommended analysis A: exclude NO_CONS from substantive complaint denominators

Re-estimate every complaint outcome after removing texts classified as no-cons statements.

The revised measure would be:

Cg,k∗=Pr⁡(k mentioned∣substantive cons text,g)C^{*}_{g,k} = \Pr(k\text{ mentioned}\mid \text{substantive cons text},g)

where gg identifies headquarters or subsidiaries.

The management and organizational differences should then be recomputed.

Interpretation:

  • If the gaps remain large, the substantive interpretation becomes stronger.
  • If they contract substantially, reporting behavior contributes to the observed differences.
  • If they disappear, the central complaint-based finding would need major revision.

This should be the first robustness analysis.

120.3. Recommended analysis B: model substantive complaint reporting separately

A two-stage approach would distinguish:

Stage 1: Whether the reviewer reports any substantive negative workplace issue.

Stage 2: Conditional on substantive reporting, which issues are mentioned?

This would separate two conceptually different phenomena:

  1. The propensity to report dissatisfaction.
  2. The thematic composition of dissatisfaction.

The distinction is important because no-cons responses may themselves provide useful information about perceived employment quality.

Simply deleting them could also remove meaningful variation.

Therefore, both the original and conditional estimands should be retained.

120.4. Recommended analysis C: review length and thematic density

Review length may influence the probability of mentioning a theme.

Longer texts have more opportunities to include multiple coded topics.

The analysis should compare headquarters and subsidiary reviews on:

  • Word count.
  • Sentence count.
  • Number of themes per pros text.
  • Number of themes per cons text.
  • Number of domains per text.
  • Frequency of generic evaluations.
  • Frequency of no-cons statements.

A particularly useful approach would estimate theme differences conditional on the total number of themes mentioned.

However, this also changes the estimand.

Controlling for review length may remove a genuine consequence of employee experience if dissatisfaction affects how extensively people write.

Therefore, I recommend reporting both adjusted and unadjusted estimates.

Priority

Very high.

The central findings are sufficiently complaint-driven that differences in reporting behavior must be addressed directly.

121. Threat 2: self-selection into Glassdoor reviewing

Glassdoor reviews are voluntary.

Reviewers are not randomly sampled from multinational employees.

The probability of writing a review may depend on:

  • Satisfaction.
  • Dissatisfaction.
  • Employment transitions.
  • Career stage.
  • Organizational conflict.
  • Recruitment experiences.
  • Expectations.
  • Workplace norms.
  • Country-specific platform usage.

Consequently, the observed sample may not represent the entire headquarters or subsidiary workforce.

121.1. Why company-job matching does not eliminate selection

Matching the same company and normalized job title controls for important organizational and occupational characteristics.

However, it does not guarantee comparable reviewer populations.

For example, headquarters reviews may disproportionately come from employees who experienced restructuring.

Subsidiary reviews may disproportionately come from employees who value the multinational's local reputation.

Even if employees hold the same title, their probability of submitting a review may differ.

This could generate apparent location effects without corresponding differences in the underlying employee population.

121.2. Recommended analysis: reviewer-composition balance

Compare the distributions of:

  • Tenure.
  • Employment status.
  • Current/former status.
  • Job level.
  • Job function.
  • Review period.
  • Available demographic variables, if any.

The comparison should occur both:

  1. In the full identified-country review dataset.
  2. Within matched company-job cells.

This would establish whether matching improves reviewer comparability.

121.3. Standardization and reweighting

A useful analysis would reweight headquarters and subsidiary reviews to a common observable composition.

For example, estimate a common distribution of:

  • Tenure.
  • Employment status.
  • Current/former status.
  • Review period.
  • Occupational level.

Then compute standardized differences.

If the main management and organizational gaps remain, observable reviewer composition becomes a less convincing explanation.

However, such adjustments cannot eliminate selection on unobserved characteristics.

121.4. Negative-control and sensitivity analyses

Potential approaches include:

  • Comparing outcomes that should be less sensitive to organizational location.
  • Examining reviewer behavior across multiple rating dimensions.
  • Testing sensitivity to unobserved selection.
  • Comparing current and former employees.
  • Restricting to regular employees.
  • Examining reviews with similar overall ratings.

The last approach requires particular care.

Conditioning on overall ratings may introduce selection or collider bias because location and workplace experiences may both influence ratings.

It should therefore be presented as a descriptive diagnostic, not a causal adjustment.

Priority

Very high.

Self-selection is a fundamental limitation of the review-based research design.

122. Threat 3: imperfect job comparability

The study matches employees within:

Company × normalized job title.

This is a substantial improvement over comparing broad country-level employee averages.

However, nominal job equivalence does not guarantee functional equivalence.

This may be especially problematic within multinationals.

122.1. Example: software engineering

A software engineer at headquarters may work on:

  • Product architecture.
  • Core platform development.
  • Global strategic projects.
  • Research and development.

A same-title software engineer abroad may work on:

  • Implementation.
  • Localization.
  • Client-specific adaptations.
  • Maintenance.
  • Regional product development.

These are illustrative possibilities, not measured differences in the workbook.

They could influence:

  • Work content.
  • Learning opportunities.
  • Management relationships.
  • Organizational exposure.
  • Career mobility.

122.2. Example: management

A headquarters manager may coordinate multiple business units.

A subsidiary manager may oversee a local operational team.

Even if the title is identical, the organizational scope may differ.

This is especially important because the strongest favorable subsidiary differences concern management and organizational functioning.

122.3. Recommended analysis A: title-quality audit

Draw a stratified sample of matched company-job cells.

Oversample:

  • Management.
  • Software engineering.
  • Consulting.
  • Marketing/product.
  • Country pairs with large effects.

For each cell, independently assess whether the title plausibly corresponds to equivalent responsibilities.

A validated title-comparability classification could distinguish:

  1. High comparability.
  2. Moderate comparability.
  3. Low comparability.

The main results should then be re-estimated using only high-comparability matches.

122.4. Recommended analysis B: task-content similarity

If job descriptions are available, compare the tasks associated with matched titles.

Potential measures include:

  • Skill requirements.
  • Occupational task categories.
  • Managerial responsibilities.
  • Client-facing responsibilities.
  • Decision authority.
  • Technical versus administrative work.
  • Global versus local scope.

The objective would be to determine whether the headquarters-subsidiary differences remain after accounting for actual job content.

122.5. Recommended analysis C: narrowly defined occupational samples

Re-estimate the results for carefully selected titles with relatively standardized responsibilities.

For example, the analysis could prioritize titles whose tasks are more clearly comparable across countries.

This would sacrifice sample size but improve construct validity.

Priority

Very high.

The research question explicitly concerns the same company and the same job.

Therefore, demonstrating the quality of job equivalence is essential.

123. Threat 4: measurement error in LLM coding

The README reports approximately 0.80 F1 agreement against alternative-model coding on 970 held-out reviews.

Agreement is reported as similar between headquarters and subsidiaries.

This is encouraging.

However, several measurement issues remain.

123.1. Equal F1 does not imply unbiased differences

Even if headquarters and subsidiary texts have similar overall F1 scores, coding errors may differ in their direction.

For example:

  • False positives may be more frequent for a particular theme at headquarters.
  • False negatives may be more frequent abroad.
  • Some culturally specific expressions may be interpreted differently.
  • Review lengths may affect classification accuracy.

These differences could influence estimated headquarters-subsidiary gaps.

123.2. Domain-specific measurement validity

The broad 0.80 F1 statistic does not establish equal performance across all 126 themes.

Some themes may be easier to identify than others.

For example:

  • Benefits references may be relatively explicit.
  • Organizational culture may be more subjective.
  • Favoritism may be expressed indirectly.
  • Management quality may overlap with employee treatment.
  • Organizational change may be described through diverse language.

Therefore, the main themes require separate validation.

123.3. Recommended validation design

Construct a stratified human-coded validation sample.

The sample should cover:

  • Headquarters and subsidiary texts.
  • Major countries.
  • Different job functions.
  • Different tenure groups.
  • Current and former employees.
  • Pros and cons.
  • Short and long reviews.

The validation should prioritize the themes driving the central findings.

These include:

  • Management quality.
  • Employee treatment.
  • Leadership communication.
  • Organizational change.
  • Job security.
  • Bureaucracy.
  • Workplace culture.
  • Benefits.
  • NO_CONS.

123.4. Measurement-error correction

The README identifies a planned correction based on Egami and colleagues' work on design-based supervised learning.

The principle is important.

Instead of treating machine-generated classifications as error-free, the analysis should use validation data to quantify and correct classification error.

The corrected estimates should include the additional uncertainty associated with theme classification.

Required output

For each central outcome, report:

  1. Original LLM-coded estimate.
  2. Human-validation-adjusted estimate.
  3. Corrected confidence interval.
  4. Estimated classification sensitivity.
  5. Estimated classification specificity.
  6. Differential error diagnostics by location.

Priority

Very high.

The theoretical contribution depends on the validity of the coded themes.

124. Threat 5: multiple testing and discovery bias

The workbook contains:

  • 126 themes.
  • 15 domains.
  • Numerous subgroup dimensions.
  • Thousands of subgroup-theme estimates.

This creates a substantial multiple-testing problem.

The probability of observing apparently significant results by chance increases when many comparisons are examined.

Moreover, the current research process is exploratory.

The important findings were identified after inspecting a large number of outcomes.

Therefore, conventional confidence intervals should not be interpreted as confirmatory evidence for hypotheses selected from the same data.

124.1. Recommended approach

Separate the analysis into:

Discovery

Identify promising patterns using the existing workbook.

Confirmation

Test a limited set of prespecified hypotheses on an independent sample or held-out portion of the data.

If independent data are unavailable, a temporal or company-level holdout could be considered.

However, such a split must be designed carefully because observations are clustered within companies.

124.2. Multiple-testing correction

For exploratory theme-level analyses, report false discovery rate adjustments.

A suitable starting point is the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

For the primary hypotheses, familywise error control may be more appropriate.

However, multiple-testing procedures should be applied to clearly defined hypothesis families.

For example:

  • Fifteen domain-level outcomes.
  • Selected management themes.
  • Selected organizational themes.
  • Geographic interactions.
  • Occupational interactions.

A single correction across every exploratory comparison may be unnecessarily conservative and difficult to interpret.

124.3. Recommended primary outcomes

I recommend preregistering a limited number of primary outcomes:

  1. Management and employee relations.
  2. Organization and business.
  3. Culture and relationships.
  4. Benefits.
  5. Overall star rating.

The remaining themes should primarily be treated as mechanism-oriented or exploratory outcomes.

Priority

Very high.

This is essential for converting an exploratory discovery into a credible confirmatory study.

125. Threat 6: unequal firm and country composition

The matched cells are weighted equally.

This means that firms contributing many distinct matched job titles receive more weight in the overall estimate.

Although equal-cell weighting is defensible, it targets a particular estimand.

It does not provide an equal-company average.

A large multinational contributing many matched titles may influence the results more than a company contributing only one or two.

Similarly, the geographic distribution is uneven.

India contributes a substantial share of the coded matched-cell observations.

125.1. Recommended weighting comparisons

Estimate the main results under:

  1. Equal matched-cell weighting.
  2. Equal-company weighting.
  3. Review-count weighting.
  4. Design-weighted review estimation.
  5. Country-balanced weighting.

These approaches answer different questions.

The study should explicitly state which population each estimate represents.

125.2. Leave-one-country-out analysis

This is a particularly important robustness test.

The first analysis should exclude India.

Then repeat the main results after excluding each major destination country.

The central question is:

Do management and organizational advantages remain when the largest host-country contributor is removed?

If yes, generalizability becomes stronger.

If no, the findings may be better interpreted as reflecting particular headquarters-host-country relationships.

Neither outcome invalidates the study.

However, the theoretical contribution would differ.

125.3. Leave-one-company-out analysis

Repeat the main estimates while excluding one company at a time.

Report the range of resulting estimates.

This would identify whether a small number of firms drive the overall results.

Although the company-spread sheet suggests broad prevalence, a formal influence analysis would be more convincing.

Priority

Very high.

126. Threat 7: headquarters origin and host-country institutions are confounded

The study is dominated by US-headquartered companies.

Consequently, a foreign subsidiary comparison often simultaneously represents:

  • A move away from headquarters.
  • A move outside the United States.
  • A change in national labor-market institutions.
  • A change in employee reference groups.

These effects are difficult to separate.

For example, the benefits disadvantage might reflect differences between US employer-sponsored benefits and employment arrangements abroad.

It might not represent a generic headquarters advantage.

126.1. Recommended analysis

Construct separate measures for:

  • Headquarters location.
  • Host-country institutions.
  • Domestic versus international location.
  • Institutional distance.
  • Economic development.
  • Labor-market wage levels.

Where possible, include non-US-headquartered multinationals.

Without greater variation in headquarters origin, the study cannot establish whether the observed pattern generalizes beyond the US multinational context.

Priority

High.

This issue is especially important for the international business contribution.

127. Threat 8: English-language selection

The README states that all coded texts are English.

This creates a potentially important selection problem.

Employees who write English-language reviews in foreign subsidiaries may differ from employees who write in local languages.

They may be more likely to:

  • Hold professional positions.
  • Work in internationally oriented roles.
  • Possess stronger English-language skills.
  • Interact frequently with multinational systems.
  • Have different educational or career backgrounds.

These attributes could influence workplace evaluations.

127.1. Implications

The coded sample may overrepresent particular employees within foreign subsidiaries.

This could contribute to the strong professional and managerial patterns.

Furthermore, English-language selection may vary by country.

For example, the composition of English-language reviewers in India may differ from that in Japan, France, or Germany.

127.2. Recommended analyses

If original-language reviews are available:

  1. Extend coding to additional languages.
  2. Validate multilingual classification.
  3. Compare English and non-English reviews within countries.
  4. Re-estimate the main outcomes using multilingual data.
  5. Examine whether language selection predicts ratings.

If multilingual text is unavailable, the paper should explicitly restrict its inference to English-language reviewers.

Priority

High.

128. Threat 9: differences in information access rather than working conditions

This threat is particularly relevant to the organizational domain.

Headquarters employees may know more about:

  • Corporate restructuring.
  • Strategic difficulties.
  • Internal organizational politics.
  • Leadership changes.
  • Financial performance.
  • Workforce reductions.

Greater information access could increase the probability that these issues appear in reviews.

This would not necessarily indicate objectively worse working conditions.

128.1. A critical test

Separate themes into two categories.

Directly experienced workplace conditions

Examples:

  • Immediate management support.
  • Employee treatment.
  • Workload.
  • Working hours.
  • Physical workspace.

Corporate-level organizational information

Examples:

  • Corporate strategy.
  • Organizational growth and change.
  • Financial performance.
  • Business instability.
  • Organizational scale.

If the subsidiary advantage is concentrated exclusively in the second category, differential information access becomes a stronger explanation.

If it also appears in direct employee-treatment and supervisory-experience themes, the evidence for substantive workplace differences becomes stronger.

128.2. What the existing evidence suggests

The overall results include fewer subsidiary complaints about:

  • Management quality.
  • Employee treatment.
  • Leadership communication.
  • Performance appraisal.

These themes are not exclusively corporate-level information topics.

Therefore, differential information access is unlikely to provide a complete explanation.

However, it could explain part of the organizational-domain advantage.

Priority

Very high.

This is one of the most important theoretical discriminating tests.

129. Threat 10: the net measure combines different processes

The net outcome is:

ΔNET=ΔPraise−ΔComplaints\Delta NET=\Delta Praise-\Delta Complaints

This is useful, but it can conceal very different patterns.

For example:

Management

Subsidiaries have approximately equal praise and substantially fewer complaints.

Benefits

Subsidiaries have substantially less praise and somewhat fewer complaints.

Culture

Subsidiaries generally have more praise and fewer complaints.

These are not equivalent psychological or organizational processes.

129.1. Recommended approach

For every primary outcome, estimate three models:

  1. Praise difference.
  2. Complaint difference.
  3. Net difference.

The net measure should remain a summary rather than replace the underlying components.

129.2. Standardized interpretation

An especially useful extension would estimate:

  • Probability of positive theme mention.
  • Probability of negative theme mention.
  • Probability of any theme mention.
  • Conditional thematic distribution.

This could distinguish:

Valence: Whether a theme is discussed positively or negatively.

Salience: Whether the theme is mentioned at all.

That distinction may become an important methodological contribution.

For example, benefits may be more salient at headquarters without necessarily being evaluated more favorably in every respect.

Priority

Very high.

131. A prioritized empirical validation program

I recommend organizing the next empirical phase into four stages.

Stage 1. Validate the central descriptive effect

Objective: Determine whether the main management and organizational advantages survive the most obvious measurement and composition concerns.

Required analyses:

  1. Remove NO_CONS from substantive complaint denominators.
  2. Report separate praise and complaint models.
  3. Apply equal-company weighting.
  4. Exclude India.
  5. Perform leave-one-company-out analysis.
  6. Restrict to current regular employees.
  7. Restrict to highly comparable job titles.
  8. Compare text and ratings on common matched cells.

Decision criterion

If the management and organizational advantages remain positive and substantively meaningful across these tests, the central empirical claim becomes substantially stronger.

If they weaken substantially, the paper should be reframed accordingly.

Stage 2. Validate theme coding

Objective: Establish that the principal findings are not generated by LLM classification errors.

Required analyses:

  1. Human-code a stratified validation sample.
  2. Estimate theme-specific classification accuracy.
  3. Test differential error by location.
  4. Correct the prevalence estimates.
  5. Re-estimate confidence intervals incorporating coding uncertainty.

Decision criterion

The strongest evidence would show that the corrected management, organization, culture, and benefits differences remain directionally consistent with the original results.

Stage 3. Test the international mechanism

Objective: Determine whether the foreign advantage exceeds the difference associated with domestic nonheadquarters employment.

Required analyses:

  1. Create common-support company-job comparisons.
  2. Estimate foreign-versus-domestic interactions.
  3. Control for sector, period, occupation, and company.
  4. Examine institutional indicators.
  5. Separate corporate-information themes from direct workplace-experience themes.

Decision criterion

If foreign subsidiaries retain substantially different managerial and organizational experiences relative to domestic establishments, the international business contribution becomes stronger.

Stage 4. Test the moderators

Objective: Determine whether geographic, occupational, and tenure differences represent genuine interactions.

Required analyses:

  1. Country × location.
  2. Function × location.
  3. Tenure × location.
  4. Employment status × location.
  5. Sector × location.
  6. Institutional characteristics × location.

These tests should use direct interaction coefficients and clustered uncertainty.

Subgroup confidence intervals alone are insufficient.

For the domestic-versus-international comparison, a three-category location variable would be particularly useful:

  1. Headquarters state.
  2. Other domestic state.
  3. Foreign country.

The model could estimate separate coefficients for domestic nonheadquarters and foreign employment.

The central statistical test would be:

H0:βForeign=βDomesticH_0: \beta_{Foreign} = \beta_{Domestic}

Rejecting this null would establish that the foreign and domestic location coefficients differ, conditional on the model and sample.

However, even this would not establish a causal national-border effect without stronger assumptions.

133. How to test the company-spread finding

The company-level distribution is one of the strongest descriptive features of the workbook.

For example:

  • 54 of 61 companies have positive management net differences.
  • 50 of 61 have positive organization net differences.
  • 42 of 61 have negative benefits net differences.

A stronger statistical analysis would estimate the distribution of company-specific effects.

Possible approaches include:

  1. Equal-company averages.
  2. Company-level bootstrap intervals.
  3. Hierarchical models.
  4. Random-coefficient models.
  5. Empirical Bayes shrinkage estimates.

The objective would be to distinguish genuine between-company heterogeneity from sampling noise.

Important issue

Company estimates based on five matched cells are much less precise than those based on several dozen cells.

A naive ranking of company point estimates would overemphasize noisy extremes.

Shrinkage estimation could help address this problem.

140. Final assessment of the research question

The original research question is:

Do working conditions differ between a multinational's headquarters and its foreign subsidiaries, for the same company and the same job?

The evidence supports a clear descriptive answer.

Yes. Employees in foreign subsidiaries report systematically different employment experiences from headquarters employees, even when comparisons are matched by company and normalized job title.

However, the differences are not uniformly favorable to one location.

The strongest findings are:

1. Foreign subsidiary employees report fewer managerial and organizational problems.

This is supported by large overall net differences, broad company-level prevalence, and higher managerial ratings.

2. Headquarters employees report stronger positive benefits experiences.

This is supported by substantial benefits-praise differences, although the interpretation as objectively better benefits remains conditional.

3. Workplace culture is generally evaluated more favorably in subsidiaries.

The finding is supported by text and ratings but varies geographically and occupationally.

4. Compensation, workload, working time, and career outcomes are heterogeneous.

Some differences reverse across sectors, functions, and countries.

5. The foreign advantage is substantially stronger than the corresponding domestic nonheadquarters difference.

This is potentially the most important basis for an international business contribution.

6. The differences appear more pronounced among longer-tenured employees.

This is promising but cannot yet be separated from survivor selection.

The evidence therefore supports a multidimensional rather than hierarchical interpretation of multinational employment quality.

141. The strongest theoretical interpretation

The findings suggest that multinational enterprises may distribute different forms of employment advantage across organizational locations.

Headquarters may offer stronger benefits-related advantages and access to certain organizational opportunities.

Foreign subsidiaries may offer more favorable managerial and relational experiences while exposing employees to different work demands and institutional conditions.

These differences may reflect three interacting processes:

  1. Organizational differentiation: Headquarters and subsidiaries perform different roles within the multinational.
  2. Institutional embedding: Employment practices and expectations vary across national environments.
  3. Occupational position: Different jobs expose employees to different organizational demands and resources.

This perspective offers a potentially meaningful extension of multinational organization theory.

It shifts attention from how firms distribute activities and capabilities to how they distribute employee experiences.

The central question becomes:

How does the international organization of the multinational shape the employment relationship experienced by otherwise comparable employees?

That is a worthwhile theoretical question.

142. Final recommendation to the research team

I recommend making the foreign-specific managerial and organizational advantage the initial empirical centerpiece.

It currently has the strongest supporting evidence.

The contrasting benefits-praise disadvantage should provide the second major dimension.

Together, they motivate a theory of differentiated multinational employment experiences.

The paper should then examine geography, occupation, and tenure as boundary conditions.

However, no definitive theory should be selected until the principal findings survive the most important measurement and selection tests.

In particular, I would make four analyses mandatory before drafting a journal submission:

  1. NO_CONS-adjusted complaint estimates.
  2. Leave-India-out and equal-company-weighted estimates.
  3. Direct foreign-versus-domestic interaction tests.
  4. Human-validated and measurement-error-adjusted theme estimates.

If the central results survive these tests, the study has the potential to make a distinctive contribution at the intersection of international business, international HRM, and organizational theory.

The most important contribution would not be that subsidiaries are better workplaces.

It would be that the same multinational organizes employment in ways that generate systematically different, multidimensional employee experiences across national and organizational locations.

That is a more precise, theoretically defensible, and potentially publishable conclusion.

Final research conclusion

The data reveal a robust descriptive headquarters-subsidiary asymmetry in reported employment experiences.

Foreign subsidiaries receive more favorable managerial, organizational, and cultural evaluations, while headquarters employees more frequently praise benefits.

The asymmetry is geographically contingent, occupationally differentiated, and potentially amplified by tenure.

It is also more pronounced across international borders than across domestic establishments.

The remaining challenge is to determine how much of this pattern reflects actual employment conditions rather than reviewer selection, thematic salience, institutional reference groups, or measurement error.

Resolving that challenge is essential to transforming the descriptive discovery into a credible theoretical contribution.
